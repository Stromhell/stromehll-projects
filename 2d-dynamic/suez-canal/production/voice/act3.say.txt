In seventeen ninety-eight, Napoleon invaded Egypt. He wanted a canal to hurt Britain's trade with India.

His chief engineer, Jacques-Marie Le Père, measured the land and came back with terrible news. The Red Sea, he said, was about ten metres higher than the Mediterranean. A canal would flood the whole Nile delta.

He was wrong. The measurements had errors. In eighteen forty-seven new surveys proved the two seas were almost exactly the same height.

Now enter Ferdinand de Lesseps: not an engineer, but a retired French diplomat. As a young man in Egypt, he had been friends with the ruler's son, Said.

In eighteen fifty-four Said became ruler of Egypt. De Lesseps rushed back, rode across the desert with him, and walked away with the right to build the canal.

Britain hated the idea of a French-run shortcut to India. Its prime minister, Lord Palmerston, called the plan impossible. British newspapers called de Lesseps a fraud.
