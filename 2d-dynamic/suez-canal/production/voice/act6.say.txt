But Egypt had paid for much of the canal and its party with borrowed money. By eighteen seventy-five, Ismail was nearly broke. He sold Egypt's shares in the canal company.

The buyer was Britain. Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli bought them in days, with four million pounds borrowed from the Rothschild bank.

Seven years later, British troops occupied Egypt. The country that dug the canal no longer controlled it.

In nineteen fifty-six, Egypt's president Gamal Abdel Nasser took the canal over. Britain, France and Israel invaded, but under pressure from America and the Soviet Union, they pulled out.

Today around one in eight of all the world's traded goods passes through the canal, and it earns Egypt billions of dollars a year.

Pharaohs, kings, emperors and sultans all tried. The desert beat most of them. In the end it took steam, debt and one stubborn diplomat. And even now, one ship stuck sideways can stop the world.
