Around 600 BC, Pharaoh Necho the Second had an idea. Ships could sail from the Mediterranean up the Nile, then along a new canal east to the Red Sea.

Thousands of farmers dug with copper tools and reed baskets under the desert sun. The Greek historian Herodotus wrote that a hundred and twenty thousand of them died.

Then an oracle warned Necho that the canal would only help foreign invaders. He stopped the work.

About a hundred years later, the Persian king Darius, who now ruled Egypt, finished it. His ships could sail from the Nile to the Red Sea.

To make sure nobody forgot, he set up giant carved stones along the bank. One says: I ordered this canal to be dug, and ships went from Egypt to Persia, as I desired.

But the desert never stops moving. Wind-blown sand and river mud slowly filled the channel, and it had to be dug out again and again.
