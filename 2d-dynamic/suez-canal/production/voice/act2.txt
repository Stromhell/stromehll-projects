After Alexander the Great, Greek kings ruled Egypt. Around 270 BC, Ptolemy the Second reopened the canal, and his engineers added a gate that stopped salty sea water flowing into the Nile's fresh water.

Some historians call it the first canal lock in history. It protected the farms of the Nile, which fed the whole country.

Rome took Egypt and kept the canal going. Under Emperor Trajan, barges full of Egyptian grain sailed along it. Rome ate Egyptian bread.

When Rome weakened, nobody paid to clear the sand. The Arab general Amr ibn al-As reopened the canal around 640, but about a hundred and thirty years later a caliph had it blocked on purpose, to cut off rebels in Arabia.

For the next thousand years, the canal was gone. Even its route was forgotten.

In the 1500s, Portugal's ships went round Africa and took the spice trade. In Istanbul, the Ottoman grand vizier Sokollu Mehmed Pasha planned a canal to fight back. The plan was shelved. The empire had too many wars.
