The real riches were on the mainland. In central Mexico the Aztec Empire ruled from Tenochtitlan, a city of perhaps two hundred thousand people built on a lake.

In 1519 Hernan Cortes landed on the coast with about five hundred men. He sank most of his own ships so nobody could go back.

He had a secret weapon: a young Indigenous woman named Malintzin, who spoke Nahuatl and Maya and soon learned Spanish. She became his interpreter and adviser.

His bigger weapon was the Aztecs' enemies. Peoples like the Tlaxcalans hated Aztec rule, and tens of thousands of them joined the Spanish.

Emperor Moctezuma welcomed the strangers into his city. Cortes took him hostage.

In 1520 Moctezuma was killed and the city rose up. The Spanish fled at night, so loaded with gold that some drowned in the canals. They called it the Sad Night.

But they had left something behind: smallpox. The people of the Americas had never met it. It killed a huge part of the city, including the new emperor.

In 1521, after a siege of about three months, Tenochtitlan fell. On its ruins the Spanish built Mexico City.

Ten years later Francisco Pizarro did something similar in Peru. The Inca Empire stretched for thousands of kilometres along the Andes, and it had just been torn by a civil war between two brothers.

In 1532, at Cajamarca, about a hundred and seventy Spaniards ambushed the emperor Atahualpa in a town square and took him prisoner.

Atahualpa offered a deal. He would fill a room with gold once, and twice with silver, if they let him go. The treasure came. They killed him anyway.

By the 1540s Spain ruled two huge viceroyalties, New Spain from Mexico City and Peru from Lima. Most of the Americas' people had died, mainly from disease.
