In 1492 Spain had just conquered Granada. Next door, Portugal was getting rich by sailing around Africa to reach the spices of Asia.

Christopher Columbus, a sailor from Genoa, had a different idea. Sail west, straight across the Atlantic, and you reach Asia faster.

The experts said he was wrong, and they were right. He thought the world was much smaller than it is. Portugal said no. But Ferdinand and Isabella, flush from victory, said yes.

On the third of August 1492 he left Spain with three small ships, the Nina, the Pinta and the Santa Maria, and about ninety men.

After more than two months at sea, with the crew close to mutiny, a lookout saw land on the twelfth of October.

It was an island in the Bahamas. Columbus believed he was near India, so he called the people he met Indians. The name stuck for five hundred years.

To stop a war with Portugal, the two kingdoms signed the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494 and split the world along a line in the Atlantic. Everything west for Spain, east for Portugal.

Columbus made four voyages and died in 1506, still insisting he had reached Asia.
