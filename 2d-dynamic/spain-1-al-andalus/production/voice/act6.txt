The promises in that treaty did not last. Three months later, Ferdinand and Isabella ordered every Jew in Spain to convert to Christianity or leave.

Tens of thousands left, many for Portugal, North Africa and the Ottoman Empire. Their descendants, the Sephardic Jews, kept the old Spanish language alive for centuries.

That same spring, in the camp at Santa Fe, the queen signed a deal with an Italian sailor who promised to reach Asia by sailing west. His name was Christopher Columbus.

In Granada, the archbishop at first tried to convert Muslims slowly. Then the hard-line Cardinal Cisneros took over. He pushed mass baptisms and burned thousands of Arabic books in the street.

Granada's Muslims rebelled. By 1502 Muslims in Castile were told to convert or leave. Those who stayed were called Moriscos, new Christians watched by the Inquisition for any sign of their old faith.

In 1609 the king ordered even the Moriscos out. Around three hundred thousand people were forced to leave Spain over the next five years.

But Al-Andalus never really left. Thousands of Spanish words come from Arabic: aceite for oil, azucar for sugar, alcalde for mayor. Even ojala, hopefully, comes from law sha Allah, if God wills.

And the Alhambra is now one of the most visited monuments in Spain.

In 1492 Spain finished one story and started another. That same year, Columbus's three small ships sailed west, and Spain was about to become the richest empire on Earth.
