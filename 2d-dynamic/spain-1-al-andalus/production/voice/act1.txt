Around the year 700, the land we now call Spain and Portugal belonged to the Visigoths, a Germanic people who had taken over after the Roman Empire fell in the west.

They ruled from Toledo, a hilltop city above the river Tagus. But the kingdom was weak. Nobles fought over the crown, and most kings were murdered or overthrown.

In 710 a noble named Roderic seized the throne. The family of the old king never accepted him.

Across the sea, the Umayyad Caliphate, run from Damascus, had spread Islam from Arabia across all of North Africa in less than a century.

Its governor in North Africa, Musa ibn Nusayr, sent his general Tariq ibn Ziyad across the strait with about seven thousand men, most of them Berbers.

They landed in 711 below a huge rock. It is still named after him. Jabal Tariq, the mountain of Tariq. Today we say Gibraltar.

A later story says Tariq burned his own boats and told his men: the sea is behind you, the enemy is in front of you. There is no way back.

Roderic marched south with a much bigger army. But at the battle of Guadalete, parts of his army turned on him or simply left. Roderic was killed, and his body was never found.

After that the kingdom fell like dominoes. Toledo, Cordoba, Seville, Merida. Within seven years almost the whole peninsula was under Muslim rule.

Many towns did not fight at all. They signed treaties. Christians and Jews could keep their churches, their synagogues and their land, as long as they paid a tax called the jizya.

Only the cold, rocky mountains of the far north held out. In about 722 a Visigoth noble named Pelayo beat a Muslim force at Covadonga, and founded the little kingdom of Asturias.

Muslim armies even crossed into France, until they were stopped near Tours in 732. The frontier settled. The Muslims called their new land Al-Andalus.
