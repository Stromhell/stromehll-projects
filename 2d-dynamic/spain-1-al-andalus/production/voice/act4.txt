Around the year 1000, the real power was a ruler's chief minister, Al-Mansur. He raided the Christian north more than fifty times.

In 997 he sacked Santiago de Compostela, the great Christian pilgrim shrine, and made Christian prisoners carry its church bells all the way to Cordoba.

But after he died, Cordoba exploded into civil war. In 1031 the caliphate was abolished. Al-Andalus broke into more than twenty small kingdoms called taifas.

The taifa kings competed with each other in poetry, palaces and war. Some even paid the Christian kings for protection against their Muslim neighbours.

In the north, the small Christian kingdoms had grown: Leon, Castile, Navarre, Aragon and later Portugal. In 1085 Castile captured Toledo, the old Visigoth capital.

Terrified, the taifa kings asked for help from the Almoravids, strict Berber warriors from Morocco. They crossed the sea, beat the Castilians, then took over the taifas themselves.

This was the age of El Cid, a Castilian knight who fought for Christian and Muslim lords alike, for whoever paid, and in 1094 took the rich city of Valencia for himself.

Later another Moroccan dynasty, the Almohads, took over. They were even stricter. The great Muslim thinker Averroes was sent into exile, and the Jewish philosopher Maimonides fled Cordoba with his family.

In 1212 the Christian kings joined forces at Las Navas de Tolosa and crushed the Almohad army.

After that the great cities fell one after another. Cordoba in 1236. Seville in 1248. The Great Mosque of Cordoba was turned into a cathedral.
