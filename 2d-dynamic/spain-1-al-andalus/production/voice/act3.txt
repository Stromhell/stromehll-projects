In 929 his descendant Abd al-Rahman the Third went further and called himself caliph, a ruler equal to the caliph in Baghdad.

Cordoba became one of the largest cities in the world. Historians think it may have had a few hundred thousand people when Paris and London were small towns.

Its main streets were paved, and at night some of them were lit. There were hundreds of public baths and running water from aqueducts.

The caliph Al-Hakam the Second collected books from across the Islamic world. Chroniclers said his library held four hundred thousand volumes. Even if that is an exaggeration, it was one of the biggest libraries on Earth.

Farmers brought in new crops from the east: rice, oranges, lemons, sugar cane, cotton, saffron. Water wheels and canals turned dry valleys green.

Paper mills appeared in Al-Andalus, among the first in Europe. Doctors, astronomers and mathematicians worked in Arabic, and their books were later translated into Latin.

In the 9th century a man named Abbas ibn Firnas is said to have strapped on wings, jumped from a height and glided, more than six hundred years before Leonardo drew his flying machines.

Muslims, Christians and Jews lived side by side. A Jewish doctor, Hasdai ibn Shaprut, served as a top minister to the caliph.

But it was not equality. Christians and Jews paid extra taxes, could not build new places of worship freely, and could not rule over Muslims. It was tolerance with rules, and those rules could get harsher.

Outside Cordoba the caliph built a whole palace city, Madinat al-Zahra, with gardens, a zoo and a hall where, it was said, a bowl of mercury made the light dance on the walls.
