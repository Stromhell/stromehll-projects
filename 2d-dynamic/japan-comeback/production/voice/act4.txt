In December 1960 Prime Minister Hayato Ikeda promised to double people's incomes in ten years. Critics called it a fantasy.

The government poured money into roads, ports, railways and especially schools. Science and engineering places at universities grew fast.

In 1950 fewer than half of Japanese children went on to high school. By the mid-1970s, more than nine in ten did.

Young people from the countryside rode special trains to city factories. Companies were so short of workers that they called them golden eggs.

But where did the money for all of this come from? Japan had no oil and no colonies. It came from families.

Japanese households saved more of their income than almost any rich country, over twenty percent by the mid-1970s. Banks and post offices lent that money to the factories.

The income-doubling plan was reached in about seven years, not ten.
