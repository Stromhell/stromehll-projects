In 1945, Japanese newspapers printed advice on how to eat acorns, sawdust and even rats.

Sixty-six cities had been burned. Around nine million people had no home. The American general who now ran the country reportedly called Japan a fourth-rate nation.

Twenty-three years later, Japan was the third-largest economy on Earth. Its trains ran at two hundred kilometres an hour, and its radios and cars were about to take over the world.

How did a country with no oil, no colonies and no army pull that off? Not with one trick. With a war next door, a few stubborn founders and millions of ordinary families.
