By August 1945, American firebombing had burned out about half of Tokyo and most of Japan's big cities. Then came the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Factories made a fraction of what they had made before the war. Food was rationed, and the ration was not enough to live on.

Families survived on the black market, where rice cost many times the official price. They sold their clothes and watches one by one. People called it the bamboo-shoot life, peeling off layers to eat, like a bamboo shoot.

In September 1945 Emperor Hirohito, whom many Japanese had been taught to see as divine, visited General Douglas MacArthur. The photo of the tall general beside the small emperor shocked the nation.

The Americans rewrote the rules. A new constitution came into force in 1947. Its Article 9 said Japan would give up war forever and keep no army.

In the villages, a land reform took land from absentee landlords and sold it cheaply to the farmers who worked it. Farmers' own village committees decided who got what.
