Most experts said Japan should not even try to make its own cars. In 1950 the United States built millions of passenger cars. Japan built about sixteen hundred.

Toyota tried anyway. In 1957 it sent the Toyopet Crown to America. On American highways it shook, overheated and broke down. Americans laughed at it.

So Toyota changed how it worked. Make only what is needed, when it is needed, and let any worker stop the line if they see a problem. That system later became famous around the world.

In 1945 an engineer named Masaru Ibuka opened a radio repair shop in a burnt-out department store in Tokyo. The next year he started a company with a young navy officer, Akio Morita.

Their first product, an electric rice cooker, was a disaster. It burned the rice or left it raw.

In 1953 they bought the right to make transistors from an American company, even though Japan's own trade ministry thought it was a waste. By 1957 they had a radio small enough to slip into a shirt pocket.

An American company offered to buy a hundred thousand radios, but only if they carried its brand name. Morita said no. He wanted his own brand on them. The brand was Sony.

And in a wooden shed in 1946, a mechanic named Soichiro Honda strapped small engines onto bicycles. When the government later tried to limit which companies could make cars, Honda ignored it.
