In twenty twelve, that job arrived. It came from the University of Toronto.

A contest called ImageNet asked computer programs to recognise objects in more than a million photos.

Most teams hand-designed the features the computer should look for. Edges, textures, shapes.

Alex Kri-zhev-ski, Ilya Suts-kever and Geoffrey Hinton did something different. They built a deep neural network and let it learn from the data itself.

Training it took an enormous number of small calculations at once. They ran it on two Nvidia gaming cards, in a bedroom.

The best rival entry got about twenty-six percent of its top answers wrong. Their network, AlexNet, got about fifteen percent wrong.

In a field where progress came in tiny steps, it was a leap. And it ran on Nvidia.

Within a few years, almost every serious AI lab was buying Nvidia chips. The hobby nobody wanted had found its customer.

Jensen moved fast. Nvidia began building whole AI computers, not just cards.

In twenty sixteen he personally delivered the first one to a small non-profit lab in San Francisco called OpenAI. He signed it: to the future of computing and humanity.
