Then there's the name. Autopilot. To most people, that sounds like a plane that flies itself. In reality, it's a driver-assist system. You're meant to keep your eyes on the road and your hands on the wheel.

America's road-safety regulator, N H T S A, spent about three years looking at nine hundred and fifty-six crashes. It found Tesla's system for checking drivers were paying attention was weak, and linked it to crashes that killed at least thirteen people.

In December twenty twenty-three, Tesla recalled more than two million cars, nearly every one it had sold in America, to update the software. The fix? More warnings. Delivered, of course, over the air. Like a phone update.

Then California stepped in. In December twenty twenty-five, a judge ruled that Tesla's Autopilot and Full Self-Driving marketing had been misleading. Tesla stopped using the word Autopilot in the state, and dropped Autopilot as a product in the U S and Canada.

And the questions haven't stopped. In twenty twenty-six, the regulator stepped up its investigation into Full Self-Driving to cover more than three million Teslas, after reports of cars running red lights and drifting into the wrong lane.
