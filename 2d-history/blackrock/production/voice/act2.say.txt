Then in nineteen eighty-six, it went wrong in a way most people wouldn't expect.

The danger everyone watched for was homeowners who stopped paying. Fink's problem was the opposite. Rates fell, and homeowners paid back early.

Picture a family with a two hundred thousand dollar loan at nine percent. Their payment is about sixteen hundred dollars a month for thirty years.

When a bank down the road offers seven percent, they take a new loan, pay off the old one, and walk out.

For whoever owned that old loan, thirty years of nine percent interest simply disappears.

Fink's desk had bet the wrong way on rates. It lost about a hundred million dollars.

The star became the man who lost a hundred million. Within two years, he left First Boston.

He later said the lesson was simple. His team had been brilliant at making money and had no real idea how much risk it was carrying.
