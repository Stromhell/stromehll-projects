New York, the early 1980s. Larry Fink is a young bond trader at the investment bank First Boston, and he is a star.

His desk helps build a new kind of product out of something very ordinary: home loans.

A bank that lends out a hundred million dollars in mortgages has to wait up to thirty years to get it all back.

So Wall Street buys those loans, pools them, and sells bonds backed by the monthly payments. Investors get the interest; the bank gets its cash back today.

Here is a simplified version of the trick that made it so profitable.

Take a hundred million dollars of home loans paying nine percent. That's about nine million dollars of interest in the first year.

Now cut the pool into three bonds that stand in a queue. When homeowners pay back their loans, bond A is paid first, then B, then C.

A is safest, so it gets the lowest rate, say six percent. B gets seven. C takes the most risk and gets eight.

Add it up and the three bonds pay out about six point seven million. The homeowners still pay nine. The gap, more than two million a year, is the packager's to share out.

Nothing was built. The same payments were just sliced and arranged. Fink's desk made First Boston a fortune doing it.
