Now for the strangest move in the whole story. Oman's Sultan Said bin Taimur is sitting on oil money, and spending almost none of it.

By 1970, roughly six hundred and seventy thousand Omanis have just two small hospitals and a handful of schools. And his own son, Qaboos, trained at Sandhurst, is kept under virtual house arrest.

On the twenty-third of July 1970, Qaboos overthrows his father in a palace coup, with British officers helping. The old sultan is flown out on an RAF plane, and he never comes back.

The family stays. Only the king changes. Two years later, Qatar does its own version. Sheikh Khalifa pushes the emir aside while he's abroad, without a shot fired.

Qaboos does what his father wouldn't. He spends the money on his people, brings in help from Britain, Iran and Jordan, and gives defeated rebels jobs in government. By the mid-seventies, the insurgency is beaten.

That's the secret rule of the Gulf. The family works like a company. Lose a boardroom fight, and you line up behind the winner. Because a civil war means everyone loses everything.
