Then oil crashes, from over a hundred dollars in twenty fourteen to under thirty by early twenty sixteen. Saudi Arabia cuts bonuses for civil servants, faces fury online, and quietly brings them back within months.

Its bigger answer is one man. In twenty seventeen, Mohammed bin Sal-mahn becomes crown prince. That November, nearly four hundred princes and tycoons are held inside the Ritz Carlton in Ree-yahd.

For decades, the Saudi royals ruled by consensus among senior princes. Now power runs through one person. Stronger, yes. But everything rests on one pair of shoulders.

Kuwait is heading the same way. In May twenty twenty-four, the emir shut down the Gulf's liveliest elected parliament and suspended parts of the constitution. Nobody took to the streets.

And then came twenty twenty-six. War between America and Iran, and thousands of Iranian missiles and drones fired at Gulf airports, refineries and American bases. Even this month, Bahrain and Kuwait were under attack again. Still, no palace fell.

But the real danger isn't a missile. In twenty twenty, the I M F warned the Gulf's roughly two trillion dollars of financial wealth could be used up by twenty thirty-four. And the world has agreed to move away from the very fuel that pays for the deal.

These kings survived by swapping protectors, swapping workers, and even swapping kings. The question for the next hundred years is what they swap when the oil money runs dry.
