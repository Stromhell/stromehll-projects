Rewind to the early eighteen hundreds. The Gulf coast is hot, poor and small. A string of towns living off pearl diving and trade, each run by its own family.

Many of those families are cousins. The Al Kha-lee-fa, who took Bahrain in 1783 and still rule it today, are related to the Al Sah-bah of Kuwait.

Then the British arrive. But mostly, they don't conquer. Britain wants a quiet sea road to India, and the sheikhs want a powerful friend to keep rivals away. So deals are signed, one by one, from eighteen twenty to Kat-ar in nineteen sixteen.

In the late nineteen twenties, Britain lets Bahrain's pearl captains bring in foreign crews, as long as each captain sends his men home after the season. Remember that little rule. It grows into something huge.

Then the pearl trade collapses, crushed by the Depression and cheap cultured pearls. But in nineteen thirty-two, oil turns up in Bahrain. And the money flows straight to the families Britain already protects.

So these weren't strong states. They were protected ones. Which raises a question. What happens when the bodyguard leaves?
