If this sounds too easy, you're not the first to think so. In 1963, a young anthropologist had the same doubt.

His name was Richard Lee. He went to the Dobe area on the edge of the Kalahari, in Botswana.

He lived with the Ju/'hoansi, people who at the time still lived largely by hunting and gathering.

The textbook story said life like this meant working every daylight hour just to survive. Lee decided not to assume it. He counted it.

For weeks, he recorded who went out for food, for how long, and what they brought back.

The result surprised everyone. Finding food took adults about fifteen hours a week. Roughly two and a half days.

If you work a normal job, you pass fifteen hours by Tuesday afternoon.

To be fair, that number is only food. When Lee later added tool-making, cracking nuts, cooking and chores, the total rose to around forty hours a week.

That's close to a modern full-time job. But the shape was completely different. No commute, no fixed shift, no boss, and the work happened with family and friends around you.

Researchers later studied other foraging groups: the Hadza in Tanzania, the Aché in Paraguay, the Martu in Australia. The numbers vary a lot. Some groups spend far longer finding food.

So the real lesson isn't a magic number. It's who owned the hours. They decided when to work, how hard, and when to stop.
