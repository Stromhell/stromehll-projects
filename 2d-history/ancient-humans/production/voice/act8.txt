That was the human rhythm for a very long time. Food in the morning, people in the afternoon, stories at night.

Then, roughly twelve thousand years ago, in the Fertile Crescent, people began planting wild grains and keeping animals.

On paper, it looked like an upgrade. More food. More control. No more tracking an antelope across the plain.

But it set off a loop. More food meant more children survived. More people needed more food. More food meant more fields, and more fields meant more work.

And then the door shut. Once a village grew too big to live off wild food, there was no going back.

Ploughing, sowing, weeding, harvesting, grinding, storing and guarding. Every season, every year.

Remember the second skeleton? The decay, the worn spine, the shorter life. This is where it came from.

Villages became towns. Towns needed potters, weavers and traders. Then cities, and with them kings, soldiers, priests and tax collectors.

And something new arrived in human history: hours that belonged to someone else, sold in exchange for food you no longer knew how to find.

Nobody stole our time in one night. It was traded away slowly, over hundreds of generations, in a deal none of us got to vote on.
