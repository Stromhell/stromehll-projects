Back at camp, it's afternoon, and the pace slows down.

People sit in the shade and talk. Kids play. Someone combs through a friend's hair while the conversation wanders.

Someone is drilling tiny holes through small shells, to string them as a necklace.

We know people did this, because at Blombos Cave in South Africa, archaeologists found shell beads about seventy-five thousand years old.

Dozens of tiny snail shells, each pierced and worn smooth where a string rubbed against it. Some were brought from river mouths kilometres away.

Gathered, carried home and drilled by hand. Not to eat. Just to look good.

And in 2008, in Hohle Fels cave in Germany, researchers pieced together a flute carved from a vulture's wing bone.

It's around forty thousand years old, and it has five finger holes.

Someone in the Ice Age made an instrument. Someone else had the time to learn to play it.

Now think about what kept you alive here. No money. No police. No hospital.

If your hunt fails, or you break a leg and can't walk for a month, the only safety net is other people.

If people like you, they feed you. If they don't, nobody owes you anything.

So those slow afternoons of talking and joking weren't wasted. They were insurance. Friendships were your bank, your hospital and your pension, all at once.
