Then the sun goes down, and the best part of the day begins.

In 2014, anthropologist Polly Wiessner published a study of how Ju/'hoan conversation changes after dark.

She had notes and recordings gathered over decades, and she split them into two piles: talk by day, and talk by firelight.

Daytime talk was practical. Food, tracks, money and trade, and a lot of complaining and gossip about who wasn't sharing.

By the fire, it changed. In the long night conversations, eighty-one percent were stories.

Stories about ancestors, about how the world began, about people far away. Long jokes that ran deep into the night.

Wiessner argued that these firelit hours helped build what makes us human: shared memory, imagination and trust in people beyond the camp.

Not in the middle of the work, but in the calm after it.
