The first clue comes out of the ground in the Middle East, the Levant and Anatolia, where some of the earliest farms began.

Over decades, archaeologists dug up skeletons from people who lived in the same region a few thousand years apart.

The first set of bones is tall and thick. The teeth are worn, but nearly free of decay. The joints are clean.

The second tells a harder story. Teeth full of cavities. A spine worn by the same motion, repeated thousands of times. A shorter life.

One was a hunter-gatherer. One was an early farmer. Take a guess.

Most people pick the strong one as the farmer. Stored grain, a roof, tools. Surely that is the better life.

It's the other way round. The tall, healthy skeleton was the hunter-gatherer. The worn-out one was the farmer.

And it isn't one odd pair. In study after study, where farming took over, average height dropped and tooth decay, worn joints and signs of hunger went up.

In parts of Greece and Turkey, men's average height fell by around fifteen centimetres after farming arrived, and took thousands of years to come back.

A skeleton works like a diary. It records how you ate, how you moved and how hard you worked, and it keeps that record for thousands of years.

And those diaries say the people with only spears were, in many ways, better fed and less worn down than the farmers who came after them.

But bones can't tell you what an ordinary day felt like. For that, we need to walk through one.
