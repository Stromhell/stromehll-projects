Mayer had five sons, and he did something clever with them. He sent them out across Europe.

Amschel stayed in Frankfurt. Salomon went to Vienna. Nathan to London. Carl to Naples. James to Paris.

Five arrows, tied together. It became the family crest: separate, you can snap one; bundled, they hold.

Brothers in five capitals meant they could move money across borders, by letter and private courier, faster and more safely than almost anyone.

They wrote to each other constantly, sometimes in Yiddish written in Hebrew letters, which few outsiders could read.

If one brother saw trouble coming in his city, the others knew within days. That information was worth a fortune on its own.

Mayer died in eighteen twelve. His will told the sons to stay united and keep the business in the family.
