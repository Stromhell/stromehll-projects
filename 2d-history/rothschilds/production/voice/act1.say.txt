Frankfurt, 1744. Jewish families are forced to live on one narrow, walled street, the Judengasse, locked in at night and on Sundays.

Jews were shut out of most trades and guilds. Money-lending and dealing were among the few ways to earn a living.

That year, Mayer Amschel Rothschild was born into a family that took its name from an old house sign: the red shield, 'zum roten Schild'.

At 13 he was sent to Hanover to train with Simon Wolf Oppenheimer's banking house. He learned how money moved between courts.

Back in Frankfurt he traded rare coins and medals, and built a client list among German nobles.

It was a good moment to be in money. Banks, stock exchanges and trading companies had grown up in Amsterdam and London over the previous century, and in 1776 Adam Smith argued that wealth could grow, not just be grabbed from rivals.

Growth needs capital. And somebody has to lend it.

His big client was Prince William of Hesse-Kassel, one of the richest rulers in Europe. In time Mayer helped manage his money.

Then France had a revolution, and Europe went to war for more than twenty years. Armies need money. Governments need loans.

Prince William had to flee when Napoleon's armies arrived in eighteen oh six. Mayer and his sons kept handling his money and his loans while he was in exile.

Napoleon's blockade tried to stop British goods reaching Europe. Smuggling around it was a booming business, and the Rothschilds were in it.
