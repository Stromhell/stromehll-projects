By nineteen eighty-four, Nike had a problem. Reebok had caught the aerobics craze and was racing ahead.

So Nike bet most of its basketball budget on one rookie: Michael Jordan. The push came from consultant Sonny Vaccaro.

Jordan didn't want Nike. He wanted Adidas. His parents, especially his mother Deloris, got him to take the meeting.

Nike offered about five hundred,000 dollars a year for five years, plus something unusual for the time: a cut of every shoe sold with his name on it.

You may have heard the NBA fined Jordan 5,000 dollars every game he wore the red-and-black shoe. The league did send a warning. Nike turned it into an ad about a banned shoe. A fine every game? No solid evidence.

Nike hoped for a few million dollars from Air Jordan in the first years. It sold more than 100 million in the first year.

In nineteen eighty-eight the agency Wieden+Kennedy came up with three words: Just Do It. Dan Wieden said he adapted it from the last words of Gary Gilmore, a murderer executed in Utah.

By the early nineteen nineties Nike had passed Reebok again and kept going.
