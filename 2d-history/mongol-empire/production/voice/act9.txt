Back on the steppe, the Mongols split into rival groups. By the 1690s the Qing dynasty of China controlled most of them.

When the Qing fell in 1911, Mongolia declared independence. In 1924 it became a communist state, closely tied to the Soviet Union, and Genghis Khan was rarely mentioned in public.

After 1990 Mongolia became a democracy, and Genghis came back: on banknotes, at the airport, and as a forty-metre steel statue on horseback, finished in 2008.

A 2003 genetic study found that about one in twelve men in a large part of Asia share a Y chromosome that may trace back to Genghis or his close male relatives.

His grave has never been found. Legends say the funeral party killed anyone who saw it, and that horses trampled the ground flat.

The Mongols killed on a scale that is hard to imagine. Estimates run into the tens of millions, though historians argue over the numbers.

They also linked East and West, protected trade, allowed religious freedom across their lands, and promoted people for skill rather than birth.

So how did about a million herders conquer the world? Discipline, speed, a system that rewarded talent, and a willingness to be ruthless that their enemies could not match.

And it began with a boy left to die on the steppe, who decided nobody would ever abandon him again.
