Over the next twenty years Temujin fought his way to the top, defeating the Tatars, then Toghrul, then even Jamukha.

In 1206, at a great assembly by the Onon River, the tribes named him Chinggis Khan, usually written Genghis Khan.

Then he did something no steppe ruler had done. He broke up the old tribes and put every fighting man into units of ten, a hundred, a thousand and ten thousand.

Men from enemy tribes served side by side. Your loyalty was to your unit and to the Khan, not to your clan.

Commanders were promoted for results, not birth. Some of his best generals, like Jebe and Subutai, came from humble families.

He adopted a script, borrowed from the Uyghurs, so that Mongol laws and orders could be written down for the first time.

A legal code called the Yassa banned stealing livestock, kidnapping women and selling Mongols as slaves.

And a network of relay stations with fresh horses let a messenger cover hundreds of kilometres in a few days. It was called the Yam.

Every soldier rode with several spare horses and carried a composite bow of wood, horn and sinew that was stronger than most bows of the time.

They could ride for days, eat in the saddle and turn to shoot while galloping away. A favourite trick was the fake retreat.
