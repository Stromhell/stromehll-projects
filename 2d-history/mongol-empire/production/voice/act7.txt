By the 1260s the empire was too big for one ruler. It split into four khanates that often fought each other.

The Golden Horde in Russia, the Chagatai Khanate in Central Asia, the Ilkhanate in Persia, and the Yuan in China.

But for about a century, merchants could travel from Europe to China across a single political world. Historians call it the Pax Mongolica.

Travellers carried a metal tablet called a paiza, a kind of passport that ordered every station to give them horses and food.

Silk, paper, gunpowder and new ideas moved west. So did something else.

In 1346 a Golden Horde army besieging the trading port of Caffa in Crimea was struck by plague. One writer claimed they threw infected bodies over the walls.

Whether or not that story is true, the Black Death spread along the trade routes and killed perhaps a third or more of Europe's people.

In China, famine, floods and rebellion grew. In 1368 a rebel leader founded the Ming dynasty and drove the Mongols out of Beijing.

The Mongols rode back to the steppe they had come from, about a hundred and sixty years after Genghis was crowned.
