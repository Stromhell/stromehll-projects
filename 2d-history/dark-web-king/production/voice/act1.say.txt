First, where was this website? Not anywhere you could find on Google.

Picture the internet as an iceberg. The tip is the part search engines can see: news sites, shops, videos.

Below the water is the deep web. It sounds scary, but it's mostly your own stuff: email, bank accounts, medical records, anything behind a password.

You'll often hear that this hidden part is ninety-six percent of the internet. Nobody can really measure that. But it is far bigger than the tip.

And right at the bottom is a small, dark corner: the dark web. Sites you can only reach with special software.

Normally, every time you visit a website, your connection carries an IP address. It can point back to you.

The software that hides it is called Tor. It stands for The Onion Router.

Your request is wrapped in three layers of encryption, then bounced through three volunteer computers called relays.

Each relay peels off one layer. The first knows who you are, but not where you're going. The last knows where you're going, but not who you are.

And here's the surprise. Tor was first developed at the US Naval Research Laboratory, to protect government communications.

Today it's used by journalists, activists and ordinary people who want privacy. Using Tor is legal in most countries. Buying illegal goods through it is not.

So Tor could hide who you were. But it left one big problem. How do you pay?

You can't buy something illegal with a credit card. The bank keeps a record, and your name is on it.

In January two thousand nine, someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto launched Bitcoin.

It's digital money that moves from one online wallet to another, with no bank in the middle and no name on the account.

But it isn't invisible. Every payment is written on a public record that anyone can read. The wallets just have codes instead of names.

Remember that. It matters later.
