Think about what makes buying drugs on a street corner risky. The seller might rob you. Or the police might be watching.

Silk Road tried to remove both risks by copying the tricks that make Amazon and eBay feel safe.

One: ratings. Every seller had reviews from buyers. Sell bad goods, and one-star reviews would sink your business.

Two: escrow. The buyer's bitcoins didn't go straight to the seller. The site held them until the buyer confirmed the order had arrived.

Three: the post. Orders went by ordinary mail, packed to look like everyday parcels, lost among millions of real ones.

And on every sale, the site took a cut.

It banned a few things, like stolen credit cards and child abuse material. But most listings were drugs, and fake IDs and hacking tools were sold too.

In June 2011, the website Gawker published a story about it. Within days, Senator Chuck Schumer called a press conference and demanded it be shut down.

The headlines made Silk Road famous.

Now the government was paying attention. And the man running the site needed a new name.
