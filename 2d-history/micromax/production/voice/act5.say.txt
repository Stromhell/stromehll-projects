Suspect number one arrived quietly: Chinese brands. Xiaomi came to India in twenty fourteen with flash sales online. Oppo and Vivo flooded high streets with shops and billboards.

They spent heavily on ads, gave shopkeepers big margins and promoter staff in stores, and offered better cameras at the same prices.

They made their own phones and had deep pockets, close ties to chipmakers, and quick access to new parts. Micromax mostly bought designs from Chinese suppliers. Now its suppliers' own brands were its rivals.

Suspect number two arrived on 5 September twenty sixteen. Reliance Jio launched with free calls and free 4G data for months.

Overnight, India wanted 4G phones. Micromax's warehouses were full of 3G stock.

Discounting that stock to clear it cost money Micromax couldn't spare.

The Chinese brands already had 4G phones ready to ship. Micromax struggled to get the latest chips and modems as quickly as global brands did.

Suspect number three: Micromax itself. It was slow to switch, spread across tablets and TVs, and kept betting on low prices instead of building its own technology.

The fall was fast. Micromax's share of the Indian phone market collapsed from the top spot to around one or two percent within a few years.

It wasn't alone. Lava, Karbonn, Intex and other Indian phone brands were squeezed in the same way.
