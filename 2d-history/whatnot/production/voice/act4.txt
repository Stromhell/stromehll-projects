So what protects the people spending the money? Whatnot did add spending limits and watch-time limits. But only in December twenty twenty-five.

And there are gaps. Ten dollars under your limit, you can still bid a hundred, and it goes through. Bids are binding, Whatnot says, like any auction. And the limit can be raised right before you hit it.

You have to be eighteen to hold an account. But with permission, a thirteen-year-old can use a parent's. And the younger someone meets gambling, the higher the risk of problems later on.

Then there's the design. You swipe to bid. A countdown ticks. The chat cheers you on, and the host is hyping the next pack before the last one has even landed.

Experts call these dark patterns: design that nudges you into choices you might not make with a clear head. Digital law expert Dr Mark Leiser says the pressure adds up to a degradation of consent.

He'd like your running total always on screen, warnings before big bids, and more friction when spending suddenly speeds up. A click, he says, shows what you did. Not whether you had a fair chance to decide.

Whatnot says its account controls let every buyer see their total spend and set limits. But it didn't answer whether it flags rapid, repeated purchases.
