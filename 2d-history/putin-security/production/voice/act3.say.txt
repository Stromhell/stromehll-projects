His long-range jet is an Ilyushin Il-96, a four-engine airliner designed in the late Soviet years.

By today's standards it is old. Most airlines replaced four-engine jets years ago. Russia keeps flying its own.

The presidential version, nicknamed the Flying Kremlin, has a bedroom, a meeting room, a dining area and, reportedly, a gym.

Reports also describe a secure communications system, defences against missiles, and an escape option.

When he flies abroad, fighter jets can escort him.

Intelligence reports even say a team of personal doctors travels with him.

And the jet is said to be one of four near-identical planes. When he travels, the others take off too, on different routes.

The trick has a weakness. Planes are easy to follow.

Russian investigative journalist Ilya Rozhdestvensky of the Dossier Center, who works from exile, points out that public flight-tracking sites log aircraft all day long.

In theory, a powerful enough enemy could go after all four at once. Which may be why Putin often prefers the train.
