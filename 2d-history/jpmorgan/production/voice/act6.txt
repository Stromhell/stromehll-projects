Bear Stearns had been on Wall Street since 1923. In January 2007 its shares traded at about a hundred and seventy dollars.

Like other investment banks, it relied on short-term loans, much of it borrowed overnight against its bonds and rolled over day after day.

That only works while lenders trust you. In March 2008, rumors spread that Bear was running out of cash.

Lenders pulled back. Clients pulled their money out. The rumors made themselves true.

According to the head of the SEC, Bear's cash cushion fell from about eighteen billion dollars on March tenth to about two billion three days later.

If Bear collapsed, the banks and funds it owed could be dragged down with it. The Federal Reserve wanted a buyer before markets opened on Monday. It turned to JPMorgan.

On Sunday, March sixteenth, JPMorgan agreed to buy Bear Stearns for two dollars a share, about two hundred and thirty-six million dollars.

Bear's shareholders, including many of its own staff, were furious. A week later JPMorgan raised the price to ten dollars a share, about one point two billion dollars in total.

For that, it got Bear's businesses, its traders and bankers, and its forty-five-storey tower on Madison Avenue.

Press estimates at the time put the building alone at about one point two billion dollars, roughly what JPMorgan ended up paying for the whole firm.

The risky part was thirty billion dollars of Bear's mortgage-linked assets. JPMorgan did not want them on its books.

So the New York Fed set up a company called Maiden Lane to hold them. The Fed lent twenty-nine billion dollars. JPMorgan lent one billion, and agreed to take the first losses.

On those assets, JPMorgan's loss was capped at one billion dollars. Wall Street's big names were falling, and the careful bank was buying them.
