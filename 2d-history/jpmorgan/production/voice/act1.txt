The name is older than the man. In 1871, a young American banker named John Pierpont Morgan went into partnership with the Philadelphia banker Anthony Drexel in New York.

In 1895 the firm took his name: J.P. Morgan and Company. It financed railroads, steel and even the US government.

In the Panic of 1907, banks across New York were failing, and the country had no central bank to stop it.

Morgan gathered the city's top bankers in his library on Thirty-Sixth Street and pushed them to agree on a rescue plan.

It worked. But one private banker had just held up the whole system. In 1913, partly because of that panic, Congress created the Federal Reserve.

The other half of today's bank is even older. The Manhattan Company, founded in 1799, grew over the next century and a half into Chase Manhattan.

In 2000 the two old names merged into JPMorgan Chase. It was huge, but it was not yet the bank that would dominate Wall Street.

For that, it needed a banker who, at that moment, was running a struggling bank in Chicago.
