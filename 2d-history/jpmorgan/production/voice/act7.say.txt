Six months later, it got much worse. On September fifteenth, two thousand eight, Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy.

That same weekend, Merrill Lynch agreed to sell itself to Bank of America. Days later, the government stepped in to rescue the insurer AIG.

Ordinary savers got scared too. Customers pulled billions of dollars out of Washington Mutual, the country's biggest savings and loan, which had made huge numbers of risky home loans.

On the evening of September twenty-fifth, regulators seized it. It is still the largest bank failure in American history.

Within hours, JPMorgan bought its banking business from the FDIC for one point nine billion dollars.

Overnight it gained more than two thousand branches, with big networks in California and Florida.

WaMu had about three hundred billion dollars in assets and around a hundred and eighty-eight billion in deposits.

JPMorgan did not take its loans at face value. It wrote them down by about thirty-one billion dollars on day one, taking the expected losses up front.

The FDIC's insurance fund paid nothing. Depositors kept their money. And in the middle of a panic, few others had the cash to bid.

Two failing giants in six months. Both times, the buyer was JPMorgan.
