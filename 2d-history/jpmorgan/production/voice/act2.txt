Jamie Dimon was born in New York in 1956 and grew up in Jackson Heights, Queens.

His grandfather, a Greek immigrant, had changed the family name from Papademetriou to Dimon. His grandfather and his father both became stockbrokers.

At Tufts University he wrote a paper about mergers at Shearson, the brokerage where his father worked. His mother sent it to the man running the firm: Sandy Weill.

Weill gave him a summer job. After Harvard Business School in 1982, Dimon turned down offers from Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and Lehman Brothers to work for Weill instead.

They worked together at American Express. When Weill left in 1985, Dimon followed him to a struggling consumer lender in Baltimore called Commercial Credit.

Dimon was its chief financial officer at thirty. Together they bought one firm after another, including Primerica, Smith Barney and Travelers.

In 1998 Travelers merged with Citicorp to form Citigroup, then the biggest financial company in the world. Dimon became its president.

But the partnership had gone sour. In November 1998, after a weekend executive retreat, Weill asked him to resign. The board backed Weill.

Dimon was forty-two. He had spent sixteen years helping build the company. Now he was out.

For more than a year, he stayed out of banking, weighing what to do next.
