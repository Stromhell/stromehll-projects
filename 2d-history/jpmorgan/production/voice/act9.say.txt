The fortress has cracked more than once. In twenty twelve, a JPMorgan trader in London built up bets in credit derivatives so big that others in the market nicknamed him the London Whale.

The trades sat in a unit that was supposed to lower the bank's risk. When the press first raised questions, Dimon called it a tempest in a teapot.

Within months, the losses had reached about six point two billion dollars.

Dimon admitted the bank had made a terrible mistake. In twenty thirteen, JPMorgan paid about nine hundred and twenty million dollars in penalties to US and UK regulators, and admitted wrongdoing.

Then came his own health. In twenty fourteen he was treated for throat cancer. In twenty twenty he had emergency heart surgery. Both times, he went back to work.

In twenty twenty-three, the old pattern came back. Silicon Valley Bank collapsed in March, and fear spread to other banks.

First Republic Bank in San Francisco lost deposits fast. On May first, twenty twenty-three, regulators seized it and sold most of it to JPMorgan.

JPMorgan took on about a hundred and seventy-three billion dollars of loans and ninety-two billion of deposits, and paid the FDIC ten point six billion.

And the government agreed to share the losses on many of those loans.

Critics say America's biggest bank keeps getting bigger every time others fail. Dimon's answer was that the government asked for help, and JPMorgan stepped up.

Fifteen years, three failing banks, and every single time, the same buyer.
