By two thousand six, America was in a housing boom. Lenders were handing out mortgages with little or no proof that borrowers could pay.

The film The Big Short, based on Michael Lewis's book, caught the mood in one scene.

A money manager meets a dancer at a Florida club. She tells him she owns five houses and a condo, all bought with loans.

She is a film character, but loans like hers were real. Many borrowers could only keep paying if house prices kept going up.

Inside JPMorgan, the warning came from a monthly report. In October two thousand six, Dimon heard that late payments on subprime loans were rising fast across the industry.

According to Fortune, he phoned the head of the bank's securitized products business, who was on holiday in Africa, and told him to start selling.

JPMorgan cut back hard on subprime, including selling more than twelve billion dollars of subprime mortgages it had made.

Now go back to our two banks, and give them a bad year. Five percent of their loans go bad. That is a five dollar loss each.

Bank A takes the hit out of its eight dollars of capital and still has three left. It survives.

Bank B only had three. After a five dollar loss, it is two dollars below zero. It cannot pay what it owes, so it fails.

That is two thousand eight in real numbers. At the end of two thousand seven, Bear Stearns had about three hundred and ninety-five billion dollars of assets on less than twelve billion of its own capital.

That is roughly thirty-three to one. Lehman Brothers was around thirty to one.

JPMorgan had about one point five six trillion dollars of assets and around a hundred and twenty-three billion of equity. About thirteen to one.
