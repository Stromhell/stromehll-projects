A bank runs mostly on borrowed money. Your deposits are really a loan to the bank. It also borrows from other banks and investors.

It pays a low rate on that money and lends it out at a higher rate to home buyers, businesses and card users. The gap between the two is its main profit.

A bank also has its own money: what shareholders put in plus the profits it has kept. That is its capital, the cushion that takes the first hit if loans go bad.

Here is a simplified example. Two banks, each with a hundred dollars of loans.

Bank A is careful. Eight dollars of its money is its own capital, and ninety-two is borrowed.

Bank B is aggressive. Only three dollars is its own. Ninety-seven is borrowed.

In a normal year, both earn one dollar of profit.

For Bank A, that is one dollar on eight of its own: a twelve and a half percent return. For Bank B, one dollar on three: thirty-three percent.

Same loans, same profit, but Bank B's shareholders earn nearly three times as much. Its share price climbs. Its bosses get bigger bonuses.

In good years, the careful bank looks slow. Under Dimon, JPMorgan was much closer to Bank A.
