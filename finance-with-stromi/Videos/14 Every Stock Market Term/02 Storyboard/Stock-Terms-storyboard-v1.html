Marketland

An original Stromi script for Finance video 14, "Every Stock Market Term Explained in 13 Minutes" (your exact title). The transcript was research only: the story, cast, examples, skits and every line are new, and the source host's name and calls to action are gone. Milo, a 24-year-old bike courier with $500 to invest, can't understand a single sentence his coworker Benny says ("went long on a blue chip, got shaken out on a dead cat bounce, shorted it, caught in a squeeze"). So he crosses into MARKETLAND, a country with its own language, with a pocket dictionary called Lexi. One chapter per month of his first year, about 75 terms, each one a stamp in his phrasebook. Skit lines in red sit between narration like Two Thursdays and Frozen Market. Circular ending: a year later Benny says the same sentence and Milo translates it. Est. narration ~11:30; with cards, breaths and SFX it lands near 13 minutes to match the title. Kept apart from 06, 10, 11, 13, 17, 18 and 22.

M061930s exchange hall (new)1:02

A grand 1930s marble trading hall with a big board. A grand-opening ribbon reads IPO. Ticker tape scrolls: PLOP 40.00 ... F ...

Narrator A public company is one whose shares anyone can buy, on a stock exchange like the New York Stock Exchange or the Nasdaq. Each gets a short ticker symbol. Ford's is just F. The first time a company sells shares to the public is its IPO, its initial public offering.

Gus Mine would be G. For Gorgeous.

M07Broker window (new)1:24

Milo at a little window labelled BROKER that turns into a glowing phone app. Behind him, shelves marked ASSET (bike, laptop) and a red bin marked LIABILITY (a phone bill).

Narrator Milo can't walk onto the exchange himself. A broker places trades for him, today usually an app. Anything tradable, like a stock or a bond, is a security. An asset is something you own with value. A liability is something you owe.

M09Share market stall (new)1:49

A street stall where shares sell like apples. Buyers on the left hold signs: 49.98. Sellers on the right: 50.02. The tiny gap between them glows.

Narrator The bid is the highest price a buyer offers right now. The ask is the lowest a seller will take. Here, forty nine ninety eight and fifty oh two. That four cent gap is the bid ask spread.

Dot So who blinks first?

M10Share market stall (new)2:06

Two big buttons on Milo's phone: MARKET (Milo slides straight across the gap) and LIMIT (a flag at $49.50, he waits on a bench). Sticky notes pop on: DAY (melts at sunset), GOOD TILL CANCELED (held by a tortoise), STOP-LOSS (a trapdoor under the price).

Narrator A market order buys now at the best price available. A limit order only buys at your price or better, so it might never fill. A day order expires at the close. A good till canceled order waits. And a stop loss sells automatically if the price falls to a level you pick.

Gus A trapdoor for my feelings.

Lexi For your money. Your feelings are on their own.

M11Share market stall (new)2:32

Narrator Volume is how many shares trade in a day. Liquidity is how easily you can get in and out. Busy stocks are liquid. Sleepy ones can be hard to sell when you need to.

Pip That's my lemonade stand.

M14Shop street by size (new)3:09

Narrator Market cap is the share price times the number of shares. Fifty million Ploppy shares at forty dollars makes a two billion dollar company. Blue chips are big, established firms. M16Ploppy Pancake Co. bakery (new)3:40

Milo flips the pancake box like a cereal box. On the back, a nutrition-style label: BALANCE SHEET (ASSETS, LIABILITIES, EQUITY). Chalk sums on the wall: $100 SALES -> $8 PROFIT = 8% MARGIN. EPS $2. PRICE $40 / EPS $2 = P/E 20.

Narrator In April, Milo reads the label. A balance sheet lists what a company owns, what it owes, and what's left for shareholders. Profit margin is how much of each sales dollar it keeps: eight cents here. Earnings per share is profit divided by shares, say two dollars. Price to earnings divides the price by that. Forty over two is a P E of twenty.

Gus Is twenty good?

Lexi Depends. Past profits promise nothing.

M17Ploppy Pancake Co. bakery (new)4:08

Three price tags hang off one share: BOOK VALUE $10 (calculator), INTRINSIC VALUE ? (crystal ball), MARKET PRICE $40 wobbling between them. A chute drips coins into Milo's hat: DIVIDEND $1.20 = 3% YIELD. Scoreboard: $500 -> $560, ROI 12%.

Narrator Book value is assets minus debts. Price to book compares the price to it. Intrinsic value is a guess at what the business is truly worth. A dividend is profit paid out in cash. A dollar twenty a year on a forty dollar share is a three percent yield. And return on investment: five hundred dollars that becomes five sixty is twelve percent.

Milo I'm rich!

Lexi You're up sixty dollars.

M18Shop street by size (new)4:35

A big umbrella building, HOLDING CO., shelters smaller shops labelled SUBSIDIARY. A giant hand with a fat stack of shares (CONTROL STOCK) pulls a lever marked DECISIONS.

Narrator A holding company makes nothing itself. It owns controlling stakes in other companies, its subsidiaries. Google's parent, Alphabet, works this way. Control stock means owning enough to steer the big decisions.

M271930s exchange hall (new)6:26

The trading hall in a storm. People throw shares in the air and run (PANIC SELLING). A giant brass lever slams down: CIRCUIT BREAKER. Everyone freezes mid-air. Cut to a calm lake: one black swan drifts in among white ones.

Narrator Panic selling is fear selling, which drops prices, which causes more fear. So US markets have circuit breakers. If the S and P 500 falls seven percent in a day, trading pauses for fifteen minutes. Thirteen percent, another pause. Twenty, it shuts for the day. And a black swan is a huge event nobody saw coming.

Ursula (bear) Everyone take a breath.

M28Market weather studio (new)6:49

A rubber squeaky toy cat (clearly a toy) bounces once a little way up, then flops lower. The price line copies it: DEAD CAT BOUNCE. Cut to Benny dropping through his stop-loss trapdoor just before the price shoots back up.

Narrator A dead cat bounce is a brief bounce in a falling price before it drops further. And Benny? His stop loss sold him at the bottom, right before a rebound. That's getting shaken out.

M30Dangerous toolbox workshop (new)7:10

A workshop with a glass case marked HANDLE WITH CARE. A reversed arrow labelled SHORT: borrow a share, sell at $50, buy back at $40 (+$10)... or at $150 (-$100). Then a crowd of shorters on a narrow bridge as the price surges and they all rush to buy (SHORT SQUEEZE).

Narrator Going long means buying because you expect a rise. Shorting bets on a fall. Borrow a share, sell it at fifty, buy it back later. At forty, you keep ten. At a hundred and fifty, you lose a hundred, and in theory there's no ceiling. A short squeeze is when a shorted stock jumps and short sellers must buy back, pushing it higher. In January 2021, GameStop went from under twenty dollars to over four hundred and eighty in a few weeks. A long squeeze is the mirror image.

Benny That's the squeeze I got caught in!

Milo Benny, you were never short anything.

M31Dangerous toolbox workshop (new)7:49

A chainsaw labelled LEVERAGE. A seesaw: Milo's $1,000 plus a borrowed $1,000. Stock +10% -> Milo +20%. -10% -> -20%. -50% -> Milo's side smashes the floor: -100%. Then two tickets, CALL and PUT, and a padlocked contract: FUTURES, MUST BUY.

Narrator Leverage is investing with borrowed money. A thousand yours, a thousand borrowed. Up ten percent, you make twenty. Down ten, you lose twenty. If it halves, your thousand is gone. Options give you the right, not the duty, to buy or sell at a set price by a set date. A call is the right to buy, a put the right to sell. Futures oblige both sides to trade.

Dot Options are a reservation. Futures are a wedding.

M351930s exchange hall (new)8:47

The hall split into race lanes: a hummingbird darting (DAY TRADING), a kangaroo bounding (SWING TRADING), a tortoise with a lunchbox (LONG-TERM). Split screen: a chartist drawing lines (TECHNICAL) vs an inspector tasting batter in the bakery (FUNDAMENTAL).

Narrator Day traders buy and sell within a single day. Swing traders hold for days or months. Most day traders lose money over time. Technical analysis reads price charts. Fundamental analysis studies the business. Value investors hunt for bargains. Growth investors pay up for fast-growing companies. And going against the crowd is called fading.

Benny Hummingbird. Obviously.

M36Share market stall (new)9:09

Milo's phone: BOUGHT 10 @ $20. Price drops. BOUGHT 10 MORE @ $10. AVERAGE $15. Then a silhouette professor (no likeness) writes on a chalkboard PRICES ALREADY KNOW while every trader reads the same newspaper.

Narrator Averaging down is buying more after a drop. Ten shares at twenty, ten at ten, and your average is fifteen. It only helps if the company recovers. And the efficient market hypothesis, linked to Nobel winner Eugene Fama, says prices already reflect public news, so beating the market again and again is very hard.

Benny I beat it last Tuesday.

Lexi And Wednesday?

Benny We don't talk about Wednesday.

M38Milo's flat, winter (new)9:38

Milo's small flat, snow outside. A calendar with a $100 coin on the 1st of each month. Prices wobble $10, $8, $5, $8, $10; shares pile higher in the cheap months. AVG PRICE $8.20 vs MILO'S COST $7.69.

Narrator Dollar cost averaging means investing the same amount on a schedule, whatever the price. A hundred dollars a month buys more shares when prices are low. Over five months at these prices, the average price was eight twenty, but Milo's average cost was about seven sixty nine.

Milo I did nothing clever and still won?

Lexi That's the point.

M39Milo's flat, winter (new)10:00

A snowball rolls downhill gathering coins: $36,000 PUT IN -> ~$122,000 AFTER 30 YEARS (at 7% a year, assumed, not guaranteed). Then a grocery bag shrinks in Milo's hand as a calendar flips ten years: $100 -> BUYS $74.

Narrator Compound interest is earning returns on your returns. A hundred a month for thirty years is thirty six thousand dollars in. At an assumed seven percent a year, never guaranteed, that could become about a hundred and twenty two thousand. And inflation works the other way. At three percent a year, a hundred dollars buys about seventy four dollars' worth after ten years.
