R061840s farmhouse (new)0:58

1840s, rural upstate New York. A small farmhouse. Rain. Mother Eliza reads a Bible by a candle. Five children. The door bursts open: Bill, in a loud checked suit, tall hat stuffed with little medicine bottles.

Narrator John Davison Rockefeller was born on the eighth of July, 1839, in Richford, a tiny town in upstate New York. His mother Eliza was strict, religious and careful with every penny.

Narrator His father, William, was the opposite. People called him Devil Bill. He travelled from town to town selling bottled cures he called medicine, and he could vanish for months.

Devil Bill Back from business, darlings!

Eliza What business, William?

Devil Bill The kind with no questions.

R071840s farmhouse (new)1:26

Bill sells a bottle to a farmer from a wagon with a painted sign: DR. BILL'S CURE-ALL (made up). The farmer sniffs it and winces. Bill pockets the coin and winks at young John.

Farmer Does it work?

Devil Bill Like a charm. What's wrong with you?

Farmer Nothing yet.

Devil Bill See? Working already.

Narrator Bill liked to boast that he cheated his own sons whenever he could, to make them sharp. He lent them money, then charged them interest. Family or not, a deal was a deal.

R081840s farmhouse (new)1:45

Night. Bill rides off down a muddy road and the wagon lantern shrinks to a dot. Eliza holds the door. John, about ten, stands in front of the younger children.

Narrator Then, in the 1850s, after he was accused of a serious crime, Bill drifted away for good. Years later he took a fake name, Doctor William Levingston, and quietly married another woman, while still married to Eliza. He kept that double life for decades.

Narrator So young John grew up as the man of the house. And he learned two things. Never depend on anyone. And trust only what you can count.

R10Cleveland trading office (new)2:16

Cleveland, 1855. Dusty dockside office of a produce trading firm. Sacks of grain, crates of pork. Sixteen-year-old John in a stiff collar walks in, hat in hand, to a tall desk with a bored clerk.

Narrator The family moved near Cleveland, Ohio, a booming port on Lake Erie. At sixteen, John took a short business course in bookkeeping, then walked the streets for six weeks asking every office for a job.

John D. I'm good with numbers, sir.

Clerk Everyone says that.

John D. Then test me.

Narrator On the twenty sixth of September, 1855, a produce firm hired him as an assistant bookkeeper. He celebrated that day for the rest of his life, like a second birthday.

R11Ledger A desk (new)2:45

Close on the red ledger. Entries tick in with tiny sounds: wages, washing clothes 10c, Sunday school 5c, gift to the poor 10c. Tag pops: PAY ~ 50 CENTS A DAY.

Narrator His pay worked out to about fifty cents a day. And he wrote down every single cent in a little red book. Wages in. Laundry out. Church collection out. He even gave part of his first pay away.

Narrator He called it Ledger A. Decades later, he still kept it in a safe, and he'd take it out to show people. It's the same habit every money app sells you today: know where every dollar goes.

R14Cleveland trading office (new)3:26

1859. John, nineteen, and Maurice Clark, round and older, shake hands under a new sign: CLARK & ROCKEFELLER, COMMISSION MERCHANTS. John counts a stack of notes: $800 saved, $1,000 borrowed.

Narrator At nineteen he started his own trading firm with an older partner, Maurice Clark. John had saved eight hundred dollars. He needed a thousand more, so he borrowed it from the only lender he knew. His father. At ten percent interest.

Devil Bill Ten percent. And I can ask for it back any time.

John D. That's not a loan. That's a hostage.

Devil Bill That's family, son.

R15Titusville oilfield (new)3:51

August 1859, Titusville, Pennsylvania. A wooden derrick. A gush of black oil sprays the sky; men in bowler hats dance in the rain of oil. Hundreds of derricks pop up across the hills like mushrooms.

Narrator That same summer, about a hundred miles east in Pennsylvania, men drilled down and struck oil. Suddenly everyone wanted in. Not for cars. Cars didn't exist yet. For kerosene, the oil you burn in lamps. It meant light after sunset, cheaper than candles or whale oil.

Narrator Cleveland, with its lake, rivers and new railways, filled up with small refineries. In 1863 Clark and Rockefeller backed a chemist named Samuel Andrews and built one of their own.

R16Cleveland trading office (new)4:20

The office at night, two desks pushed apart. Clark waves a newspaper about debt. John calmly stacks loan papers higher and higher.

Clark You've borrowed again! We'll be ruined!

John D. We'll be bigger.

Clark Bigger and ruined!

Narrator John wanted to borrow and grow. Clark wanted to stay safe. By early 1865 they agreed to split up, and to sell the refinery to whichever of them bid the most.

R17Auction room (new)4:37

A small auction room, one gas lamp. A lawyer with a gavel between them. Price cards flip on a board: 500... 5,000... 50,000... 72,000. Clark sweats. John sits perfectly still, hands folded.

Lawyer Fifty thousand, Mister Clark. Mister Rockefeller?

John D. Sixty.

Clark Seventy two thousand!

John D. Seventy two thousand five hundred.

Clark Take it, John. It's yours.

Narrator Seventy two thousand five hundred dollars, at twenty five years old. Much of it borrowed. He later said that was the day that decided his career.

R20Riverside refinery (new)5:15

A conveyor of products pops out of the waste jar one by one: candle wax, axle grease, machine oil, petroleum jelly, fuel. Then a cooperage: workers make barrels from his own oak logs. A wagon train with his name on the side.

Narrator Every middleman he cut out was a cost he didn't pay. So he could sell cheaper than rivals and still make more profit. By 1870 his refinery was the biggest in Cleveland. Still, he wasn't satisfied.

5. The Secret Deal

R22Cleveland trading office (new)5:47

January 10, 1870. A new brass plate is screwed onto a door: THE STANDARD OIL COMPANY. John, now 30, side whiskers, stands with a few partners holding a share certificate.

Narrator On the tenth of January, 1870, he and his partners formed a new company. The Standard Oil Company of Ohio. The name was a promise: oil of one reliable standard, the same in every lamp, so it wouldn't explode in your kitchen.

Narrator But Cleveland had dozens of refineries fighting over the same customers, and prices kept collapsing. To Rockefeller this wasn't healthy competition. It was chaos. And chaos was waste.

R23Railroad office (new)6:14

A railroad boss's office: map of tracks on the wall, model locomotive on the desk. The Railman, huge moustache, cigar. John sits small but steady, opens his ledger.

Railman Why should I give you a discount?

John D. Because I'll fill your trains. Every day. Same time. No surprises.

Railman Nobody ships that much.

John D. Nobody does yet.

Narrator Railways hated empty trains. A big, steady customer was gold. So Standard won secret discounts on every barrel it shipped. They're called rebates. Smaller refiners paid the full price.

R24Railroad office (new)6:35

A dark side door. A shell company plaque: SOUTH IMPROVEMENT CO. A sheet of paper with three stamps slams down: REBATE, DRAWBACK, REPORTS. A coin flies from a rival's barrel into John's pocket.

Narrator Then, in 1872, came something far darker. A secret pact between big refiners and the railroads, run through a company with the harmless name of the South Improvement Company.

Narrator Its members would get rebates on their own oil. They would get drawbacks, a cut of the money rivals paid to ship theirs. And the railways would hand over shipping reports on what competitors were sending, and where. Your competitor pays the train, and part of his ticket lands in your pocket.

R25Small family refinery (new)7:06

A tiny family refinery. Ned, the owner, in an apron, reads a letter. His wife and kid behind him. John at the door, polite as a churchgoer, hat in hand, holding an offer sheet: CASH OR STANDARD SHARES.

John D. I'd like to buy your refinery.

Ned It's not for sale.

John D. Everything's for sale. The question is whether you sell it this year, or the bank sells it next year.

Ned Is that a threat?

John D. It's a forecast.

Narrator In roughly six weeks in early 1872, Standard bought out twenty two of its twenty six Cleveland rivals. Some took Standard shares and got rich. Many took a low cash price and never forgave him. History calls it the Cleveland Massacre.

R26Titusville oilfield (new)7:36

Pennsylvania oil town at night. Angry oil men march with torches and banners: DOWN WITH THE OCTOPUS. A newspaper flies up: CHARTER CANCELLED. Then a slow push on John in Cleveland, calmly turning a ledger page.

Narrator When the secret deal leaked, the oil regions exploded. Crowds marched, and Pennsylvania cancelled the South Improvement Company's charter within weeks. It never shipped a single barrel. The public thought they had won.

Gus We beat him!

Pip He already bought Cleveland, Gus.

Gus ...We beat him slightly.

R28War-room map (new)7:57

A big war-room table map of the eastern US, made of paper and string. Little refinery pins in every city. John's hand moves Standard's flags across it, one by one. Barrels, rail cars and acid jars get Standard labels.

Narrator Over the next few years he did the same thing city by city. Pittsburgh, Philadelphia, New York, the oil regions. And he bought the things every refiner needed. Barrel makers. Storage tanks. Chemical works. Tank cars on the railway.

Narrator A rival could still refine oil. He just might struggle to find barrels, a train, or a buyer. This is called vertical integration: owning every step from the ground to the customer.

R29Tidewater pipeline hills (new)8:25

A long new iron pipeline snakes over green Pennsylvania hills toward the coast: TIDEWATER (painted on the pipe). Workers cheer. Across the hill, Standard men stand on a strip of land with a FOR SALE: SOLD sign.

Narrator Independent producers fought back with a brand new idea. In 1879 they built a pipeline about a hundred and ten miles long, pumping crude over the mountains so they didn't need his railways at all.

Narrator Standard fought it with lawsuits and land buying, then built pipelines of its own. Within a few years it had bought a big stake and struck a deal with the rebels. By around 1880, Standard refined about nine out of every ten barrels of oil in America.

R3026 Broadway boardroom (new)8:56

26 Broadway, New York, 1880s: a grand stone building. Inside, a long boardroom with nine chairs. A tall stack of 40 company certificates gets dropped into one big wooden box labelled TRUST. The lid shuts. Nine keys.

Narrator But there was a problem. At the time, a company in one state couldn't easily own a company in another. So in 1882 his lawyers invented something new. The owners of around forty companies handed their shares to nine trustees, who ran it all as one. The Standard Oil Trust.

Reporter Who owns this refinery?

Lawyer A company.

Reporter Who owns that company?

Lawyer A trust.

Reporter And who owns the trust?

Lawyer It's... trustees all the way down.

7. The Thirty Ninth Drop

R32Tin-can works (new)9:28

A tin-can factory line. Five-gallon kerosene cans roll past a worker with a solder iron. Silver drops fall onto each lid; a counter ticks 38... 39... 40. John watches with a stopwatch, says nothing.

John D. How many drops of solder to seal each can?

Worker Forty, sir.

John D. Try thirty eight.

Worker They leak, sir.

John D. Then try thirty nine.

Narrator Thirty nine worked. One drop of metal, on millions of cans, saved thousands of dollars in the first year alone. That was the whole Rockefeller method. Tiny savings, at enormous scale.

R33Lamp montage (new)9:48

Montage: a dark poor kitchen lights up with a cheap kerosene lamp. Blue tins on a ship to Asia. Price tag on a lamp oil can drops: 26c... 15c... 8c. Families reading at night.

Narrator And here's the twist that makes him hard to judge. All that efficiency made kerosene much cheaper. A gallon fell from around twenty six cents in 1870 to about eight cents by the mid 1880s. For millions of ordinary families, the night got longer. They could read, sew and study after dark.

Narrator Standard sold its oil around the world, from Europe to China, even selling cheap little lamps so people would keep buying the fuel. Sell the lamp cheap, sell the oil forever.

R3426 Broadway boardroom (new)10:20

The old man again, younger here at 60. He removes his hat in a mirror: no hair at all, not even eyebrows. Three wigs on stands: short, medium, long. A bowl of milk and crackers on the desk.

Narrator By the 1890s the strain showed. Rockefeller stepped back from running the business. Around 1901 an illness made him lose all his hair, even his eyebrows. He ate plain food for his stomach, and later wore wigs.

John D. Which wig today?

Butler The medium, sir. It's been two weeks.

John D. Good. People will think it grew.

R36McClure's newsroom (new)10:44

1902, a magazine office: McClure's. Ida Tarbell, sharp, glasses, at a desk buried in railroad contracts, red string connecting documents on a wall. A photo on her desk: her father by a small oil derrick.

Narrator Then came Ida Tarbell. She grew up in the Pennsylvania oil country. Her father was one of the small independent oil men squeezed by the railroad deal of 1872. She never forgot it.

Narrator For years she dug through court records, contracts and testimony, and interviewed people inside the business. From 1902, McClure's magazine published her investigation in instalments. In 1904 it became a book: The History of the Standard Oil Company.

Narrator It was careful, it was devastating, and it made Standard Oil the most hated company in America.

R40Supreme Court 1911 (new)11:41

The US Supreme Court, 1911: marble columns, nine judges in black. A giant Standard Oil oil tank on a cart in the middle of the room. A gavel bangs: the tank cracks into 34 smaller tanks that roll in every direction.

Narrator President Theodore Roosevelt went after the big trusts. In 1906 his government sued Standard Oil under the anti-monopoly law. On the fifteenth of May, 1911, the Supreme Court ruled: Standard Oil had to be broken up. Into thirty four separate companies.

Teddy Roosevelt Bully! The octopus is chopped!

Narrator Some of those pieces are still household names. They grew into companies like ExxonMobil, Chevron, and parts of BP. The world thought Rockefeller had finally lost.

R411916 porch (new)12:09

A pie chart made of 34 little oil tanks. A hand labels a quarter of each: JOHN'S SHARE. Then each tank inflates like a balloon. Stock ticker tape climbs over the porch.

Narrator But here's what most people missed. He wasn't forced to sell his shares. He owned roughly a quarter of the old Standard. So now he owned roughly a quarter of each of the thirty four new companies.

Narrator Once they were separate, investors could see how valuable each piece was. Their share prices shot up. And the world was switching from lamps to cars, which ran on gasoline, the stuff refiners used to dump in the river.

R421910s exchange floor (new)12:38

A 1910s stock exchange floor. Gus the trader stares at the board as prices jump. He drops to his knees in prayer.

Gus Please, Lord. Break up something else.

Pip That's not how prayer works, Gus.

Narrator By 1916 the newspapers put his fortune at a billion dollars. Historians who've checked the numbers think his paper wealth peaked closer to nine hundred million, around 1913. Either way, compared with the American economy back then, it was about two percent of everything the country produced in a year. No American fortune before or since has matched that share.

R45Lab, university, hospital (new)13:27

A bright laboratory, then a school classroom, then a hospital ward. Banners: UNIVERSITY OF CHICAGO (1890), ROCKEFELLER INSTITUTE (1901), ROCKEFELLER FOUNDATION (1913). A microscope over a hookworm slide.

Narrator But his giving went far beyond dimes. He paid to found the University of Chicago. He created a medical research institute and the Rockefeller Foundation. His money funded a campaign against hookworm across the American South, and his foundation's scientists later developed a vaccine for yellow fever.

Narrator By the end he had given away more than half a billion dollars. Was it guilt, faith, or reputation? Probably all three. He'd been giving away part of his pay since that very first ledger.

Narrator As a young man, he reportedly said he had two goals: to make a hundred thousand dollars, and to live to a hundred. He died on the twenty third of May, 1937, at ninety seven. He missed one goal by three years. He beat the other one by about ten thousand times.

R48Stromi desk14:22

Stromi the mascot at his desk with a little red notebook. Three stamps slide in one by one: a red ledger, a single silver drop, a stack of 34 tiny tanks.

Stromi You're not going to monopolise oil. Please don't. But three of his habits are free to copy.

Stromi One: Ledger A. Write down where your money goes for one month. Most people find a leak in the first week.

Stromi Two: the thirty ninth drop. Small regular costs, multiplied over a year, are big costs. One unused subscription is your fortieth drop.

Stromi Three: own slices, not just one thing. After the break-up he owned a piece of thirty four companies. A broad index fund does that for you today with hundreds. And yes, some of Standard's descendants are in there. You might already own a crumb of Rockefeller's empire.

R49Stromi desk15:03

Stromi turns the notebook around; a fourth stamp appears with a red X over an octopus.

Stromi And one warning. Cheap kerosene was real. So was the squeeze. When one company controls the market, it sets the rules, and the price. That's why breaking up monopolies still matters for your wallet.

R501916 porch (new)15:17
