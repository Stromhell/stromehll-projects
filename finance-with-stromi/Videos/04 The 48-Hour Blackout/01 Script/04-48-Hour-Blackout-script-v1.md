## What changed vs the reel
1. The reel stacks three outages as one "global system failure". They were three separate failures with no sign of an attack (FAA, Microsoft and Workday all said so). We keep the drama but tell the truth: three different accidents, one common thread (each broke during an upgrade or a test).
2. The reel's on-screen "Microsoft down, 70+ countries, 24 hours": the outage actually ran from ~1 PM ET Thu 12 Nov until Microsoft said service was restored on Sat 14 Nov (about two days).
3. The reel's "Workday down, 700 organisations": reporting says about 11,500 businesses use the platform and were affected; we use 11,500.
4. The reel's "within six hours, American skies were empty": the ground stop ran 3:54 PM to 8:15 PM ET (about 4 h 20 min), departures only; planes already flying landed normally.
5. Added the market story the reel doesn't have: airline shares, Microsoft flat, the $50 billion ask, the CrowdStrike 2024 precedent, and what Monday's open could bring.
6. Dropped the reel's 9/11 death-toll card. We mention 9/11 only as the date of the first-ever full shutdown of US airspace.

## CHAPTER 2: THURSDAY, 1 PM: THE INBOX GOES DARK (1:50-4:00)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: Thursday, just before one p.m. Eastern. Microsoft's email system, the engine behind Outlook, starts failing. Not in one city. In the US, in Europe, across Asia-Pacific. More than seventy countries.

NARRATOR: For a twelve-person firm in the year-end rush, two days without email is two days of invoices not sent, which means money not coming in. Keep that in mind. Small delays in big systems become real dollars in small businesses.

## CHAPTER 3: FRIDAY, 3:54 PM: EVERY PLANE SITS DOWN (4:00-6:20)
[Chapter card]

VISUAL: Phoenix airport, gate area. Jimmy is eating an overpriced pretzel. The board above him is green, ON TIME.
NARRATOR: Friday. Jimmy's at the gate. Boarding in twenty minutes. At three fifty-four p.m. Eastern, the Federal Aviation Administration does something it almost never does. It orders a nationwide ground stop. No departures. Anywhere. Citing "extraordinary circumstances" and "reasons of safety."
[SFX: board clack, a hundred phones buzzing at once]
SKIT (the gate. The GATE AGENT picks up the PA mic.)
GATE AGENT: Ladies and gentlemen, the FAA has stopped all departures across the United States.

NARRATOR: Here's what was happening behind the curtain. Air traffic controllers talk to planes, and to each other, over a private communications network. NARRATOR: Planes already flying landed normally. But nothing new took off for about four hours and twenty minutes, until eight fifteen that night.
VISUAL: A US map at night, like the reel's, but in our style: a paper map on a corkboard, hundreds of little glowing pins. One by one, the pins stop moving.
NARRATOR: The damage: more than four thousand seven hundred flights cancelled on Friday alone, and around ten thousand delayed. Hundreds diverted. And because planes and crews were now in the wrong cities, the mess rolled into Saturday, with thousands more cancellations. **[CHECK AT RECORD: final Fri + Sat cancellation totals, FlightAware]**
SKIT (Phoenix terminal, night. Jimmy on the floor, using his garment bag as a pillow, on the phone.)

## CHAPTER 4: THE ROUTER FROM 2003 (6:20-8:40)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: So what broke? Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy explained it the next day.
VISUAL: Quote card, typewriter text on cream card: a router "started flooding other routers in our air traffic control system." Sean Duffy, US Transportation Secretary.
NARRATOR: The FAA has been swapping old copper phone lines for modern fiber. During that work, one router started flooding the routers around it. Duffy said the system was seeing "over one thousand connection attempts every four to five seconds."
SKIT (a dusty equipment closet. The OLD ROUTER, a beige box with blinking eyes and a cardigan, sits on a shelf. Wendell plugs a shiny fiber cable into it.)
WENDELL: Good news, buddy. You're getting fiber.
OLD ROUTER (creaky): Fiber? I was built in two thousand three. I eat copper.
WENDELL: It's faster.
OLD ROUTER (eyes spinning faster and faster): HELLO? HELLO? HELLO? IS ANYONE THERE? HELLO? HELLO?
(Every router on the shelf starts shouting HELLO back. Sparks.)

NARRATOR: According to CBS News, the problem was limited to one model of Cisco router made between 2001 and 2006. Duffy blamed contract work by L3Harris, the FAA's long-time communications contractor. The FAA switched off the faulty function on about sixty of those routers across the country.
NARRATOR: Think about that. The machines guiding the busiest airspace on earth were partly running on routers older than the first iPhone.
NARRATOR: And this wasn't a surprise. In September, the Government Accountability Office, Congress's watchdog, warned about the FAA's ageing copper cables, some dating back to the 1960s, and its slow progress replacing them.

NARRATOR: Duffy's answer is money. He's asking Congress for fifty billion dollars in emergency funding to rebuild the air traffic control system. Fifty billion dollars is about a hundred and fifty dollars for every person in America.

NARRATOR: Markets tend to punish the companies stuck with the bill, like airlines, more than the companies that caused the problem. Because when one company runs the pipes that everyone needs, a bad week doesn't change who everyone needs.

Workday, whose outage started after Friday's close, so Monday is the market's first chance to react. **[CHECK AT RECORD: WDAY, airline and MSFT moves on Mon 16 Nov]** And the companies that might be paid to fix it.
NARRATOR: Remember the fifty billion dollars? If Congress agrees, that money flows to the companies that build radar, radios, fiber and routers. A failure like this is a disaster for travellers. For some suppliers, it's a sales pitch.

## CHAPTER 8: ONE WELL, WHOLE VILLAGE (14:30-16:05)
[Chapter card]

VISUAL: A painted village square. One stone well in the middle. Every house has a little pipe running to it.
NARRATOR: Here's the big idea under all three stories. Imagine a village where every family gets water from one well. It's efficient. One well is cheaper to dig, cheaper to clean, easier to manage than a hundred. Until the day somebody drops a boot in it.
SKIT (village square. Dot leans over the well, a boot falls in. Plop. Every tap in every house goes dry at once. A row of villagers turn and stare at Dot.)
DOT: ...It was a very small boot.

NARRATOR: That's modern business. A huge share of the world's offices run their email on one company. Thousands of employers run payroll through one company. The whole US sky is managed through one network. Each of those choices made sense on its own. Cheaper, simpler, easier. But together they create what engineers call a single point of failure. One boot, whole village.
NARRATOR: And it shows up in your investments too. If you own a simple index fund, the kind that holds the five hundred biggest American companies, a big slice of your money sits in just a handful of giant tech firms. Including the one whose email went down this week.
SKIT (Jimmy's parents' living room. JIMMY'S DAD, holding a tiny pie labelled S&P 500. Most of the pie is one giant slice labelled BIG TECH.)
JIMMY'S DAD: I'm diversified. I own five hundred companies.
JIMMY (pointing): Dad, a huge chunk of your pie is, like, ten of them.
JIMMY'S DAD (squinting at pie): ...It's a very big slice.
JIMMY: That's the point.

NARRATOR: That isn't a reason to panic. These companies are huge for a reason. It's just worth knowing that "diversified" and "spread out" aren't always the same thing.

## YOUR WALLET: FOUR QUESTIONS (16:05-17:00)
[Chapter card. VISUAL: index cards pinned one by one on a corkboard.]

NARRATOR: So what do you actually do with this? Four questions.
NARRATOR: One. If your paycheck arrived a week late, could you still pay rent? A small cash cushion, even a few hundred dollars, turns a payroll outage from a crisis into an annoyance.
NARRATOR: Two. If your flight gets cancelled, do you know your rights? In the US, if an airline cancels your flight and you choose not to travel, you're entitled to a cash refund, not just a voucher. Ask for it.
NARRATOR: Three. Run a business? Where does it all live? If your email, your files and your invoices all sit with one provider, write down what you'd do for two days without it. Nina's fax-machine uncle is not a plan.
NARRATOR: Four. Look inside your index fund. Find out how much of it is in the top five or ten companies. You might be happy with the answer. You should still know it.
