The way we think about money doesn't always make sense. You might spend $100 on something without giving it much thought, then hesitate over putting that same $100 into savings. You might feel comfortable seeing money in your bank account while paying interest on a credit card balance. And sometimes a decision that seems perfectly reasonable at the time turns out to be a poor choice when you look at your finances as a whole. Why does that happen? It turns out we don't always think about money in purely financial terms. Where the money comes from, what we've set it aside for, and how we feel about the decision can all affect what we do with it. We might think we're being logical when in reality, we're overlooking something important. And this isn't just a problem for people who struggle with money. You can have a good salary, understand how interest works, and know the basics of investing, yet still make decisions that don't serve you well financially. Because understanding money and making good decisions with it aren't quite the same thing. The good news is that once you recognize some of these patterns, you can start making more deliberate choices. It's not just about controlling your spending or following a set of money rules. It's about understanding the mental processes behind your decisions and learning how to make those decisions work in your favor. Because once you understand the psychology of personal finance, you can start changing the way you manage money without constantly second-guessing yourself. But first, consider this. If $100 is worth exactly $100, why does the way you receive it or what you intend to do with it change how you're willing to spend it? We do this even though money is financially interchangeable. A dollar from your salary can pay the same bill as a dollar from a gift. If everyone suddenly gets more money, but there's still only one painting, the price goes up. That's what happened after 2020. Governments sent out trillions in relief. Interest rates were near zero. The amount of money in the US economy grew by about 40% in roughly 2 years. Engine two. Something gets scarce or expensive to make. Factories closed. Ships got stuck. Chips ran out. Fewer goods, higher prices. Engine three, expectations. If everyone believes prices will rise, workers ask for bigger raises and stores raise prices in advance. And those beliefs can become self-fulfilling. Put all three together and in June 2022, US inflation hit 9.1%. The highest in 40 years. So, how do you cool it down? The Fed's main tool is interest rates. raise rates and borrowing gets expensive. Mortgages, car loans, credit cards, people and businesses spend less. Demand cools. Price increases slow down. From 2022 to 2023, the Fed raised rates faster than at any time in four decades. It worked slowly. By 2025, inflation was back under 3%. Then this year, a different engine kicked in. Economists watch core to see what's driven by shocks and what's spreading through the whole economy. An oil shock is like a fever. The question is whether it becomes an infection. If oil prices stay high, they leak into everything. Shipping, plastic, fertilizer, airline tickets. Paul's bus company pays more for fuel. Delivery trucks charge stores more. Stores charge Paul more. And the Fed faces a hard choice. Fight inflation with higher rates or hold back to protect jobs. Here's the question Paul really wants answered. Gas went up. Gas can come down. That's because technology keeps making them easier to produce. But food, rent, and services rarely work that way. Once a bakery raises its price and customers keep paying, there's little reason to cut it. wages, rent, and contracts were all reset at the higher level. Businesses earn less, they cut jobs, prices fall more. In the Great Depression, US prices fell by about a quarter in 4 years. It was a disaster. So, the realistic best case isn't lower prices, it's wages catching up. And there's a sneaky version of inflation, too. Shrinkflation. Same price, same package, a little less inside. So, who wins and who loses? The biggest loser is cash sitting still. There's a quick rule. Divide 72 by the inflation rate and you get the years it takes for prices to double. At 3% inflation, your cash loses half its buying power in about 24 years. Here's the hidden math on a savings account. If it pays 1% interest and inflation is 3%, you're actually losing about 2% a year. Economists call that your real return. It's the only return that matters. A dollar from early 2020 buys about 77 cents worth today. People on fixed incomes get squeezed, too. A pension that doesn't adjust buys less every single year. Social Security is different. It gets a cost of living raise every year to keep up with prices. In 2023, that raise was 8.7%, the biggest in four decades, but it only catches up after prices have already gone up. Retirees always feel at it first. Now, the winners, anyone with a fixed rate loan, your mortgage payment stays the same while your paycheck and the prices around it rise. Paul and Anne locked in their mortgage years ago. Inflation is quietly shrinking that debt. Owners of real things like homes and businesses often see their value rise with prices, which also means young people trying to buy their first home get hit twice. Higher prices and higher interest rates. And the biggest borrower of all, the government. Inflation shrinks the real value of national debt, which is one reason governments attempted to tolerate it. Workers are in the middle. When inflation jumps, wages usually lag behind. In 2021 and 2022, prices rose faster than most paychecks. That's why it felt so bad. Now, how bad can it get? In the 1970s, America lived through years of high inflation. It peaked at nearly 15% in 1980. Washington even tried freezing wages and prices by law in 1971. It didn't work. When the freeze ended, prices jumped again. To stop it, Fed Chair Paul Vulkar raised interest rates to around 20%. Mortgage rates hit 18%. Two recessions followed, but inflation finally broke. That's why today's Fed raises rates when inflation jumps. It's painful medicine, but they remember the 70s. And then there's hyperinflation. Germany, 1923. Prices were doubling every few days. People were paid twice a day and ran to spend it. Zimbabwe 2008. Prices spun so far out of control, the country ended up printing a $100 trillion note. Hyperinflation almost always has the same cause, a government printing money to pay its bills. That's why countries keep central banks independent from politicians. It's a guardrail. So, what does all this mean for someone in their 40s or 50s? First, think in real numbers. A 2% raise in a 3% year is a pay cut. When you ask for a raise, know the inflation number. It's your starting point, not a bonus. Second, cash is for emergencies, not for decades. A few months of expenses in cash is a safety net. 20 years of savings in cash is a slow leak. Money parked for 20 years needs to grow faster than prices or it shrinks. Third, some savings products are built for this. US Treasury Ibonds and TIPS are adjusted for inflation by design. They won't make you rich, but they're designed not to fall behind prices. Fourth, know your debts. Fixed rate debt gets lighter with inflation. Variable rate debt, like credit cards, gets heavier. In a high inflation period, paying off a credit card at 20% interest beats almost any savings account. And fifth, don't make big decisions based on one scary headline. Inflation moves in waves. What matters is the long run. This video is just general education. For your own situation, it's worth talking to a professional. That night, Paul sticks the old receipt on the fridge. Not because he's angry, because now he understands it. Inflation isn't a thief that broke into his kitchen. It's the slow, steady cost of an economy that keeps growing and sometimes overheating. The trick isn't to stop it. It's to make sure your money keeps up because prices will keep rising. The only question is whether you rise with them. 21. The Biggest Myths in Personal Finance

There are some persistent myths in personal finance that lead people to make objectively bad financial decisions. For example, you should save as much as possible when you're young to benefit from compounding. It's almost foundational advice in personal finance, but it's wrong or at least it's incomplete. I'm going to explain why and debunk nine more myths like this one to help you make better financial decisions. I'm Ben Felix, chief investment officer at PWL Capital, and I'm going to tell you about the biggest myths in personal finance. You should save as much as you can when you're young to benefit from compounding is taken as an absolute truth in personal finance. It sounds sensible enough and like many myths does have elements of truth. A longer time horizon makes compound interest more powerful and to be sure compounding is an important tool for building wealth at long horizons. But this strategy neglects an even more important consideration. When you're young, your income is in the vast majority of cases at its lowest point and will likely steadily rise over time as your career progresses before tapering off as you approach retirement. Similarly, when you're young, it's likely that your standard of living will be at its lowest point when compared to your peak earning years and retirement. At this stage, the marginal utility, that's like the amount of additional satisfaction you can generate of every dollar you spend on improving your standard of living is at its highest. An additional $5,000 or whatever spent at age 25 may mean living in a safer area, eating better food, getting a better education, driving more reliable car, or forming core memories and experiences that you will carry with you forever. At age 45, that $5,000, even when you account for the potential investment growth in the interim, yields a much smaller increase to your standard of living. That means if you're saving as much as you possibly can when your young, when your income and standard of living are comparatively low, you're sacrificing more of what matters when you can least afford it. You're effectively robbing from the poor, your current low-income self, relatively low-income self, and giving to the rich, your future higher income self. Another way to think about this is that money is not the only thing that compounds over time. Skills, experiences, and health compound, too. Focusing only on wealth accumulation, miss is the bigger picture. This idea comes from one of the best supported models in economics called the life cycle model or life cycle hypothesis. The fundamental premise of the life cycle model is that people want to maintain a consistent standard of living throughout their lives. That consistent standard of living is really the key to the model. The life cycle model suggests that you should aim to roughly even out your standard of living across high and low income years. This is called consumption smoothing. Since income typically starts low when you're young and rises throughout your career, your saving strategy should match that pattern. Save what you can early on without sacrificing quality of life or strategically use leverage if your risk profile allows, I'll come back to that later, then increase your savings as your income grows. There's a lot more to unpack here like risk management and habit formation, but getting deeper into that, I think, needs a dedicated video. When you model this out calibrated to someone living in the United States, it's possible to show that it's optimal for people to start saving later in life when their income is higher, rather than stressing themselves out when they have to choose between saving and eating good food or whatever other trade-offs they might be making. Don't get me wrong here, saving is certainly important and so is making sure that your spending isn't frivolous, but the strict idea that you need to make sacrifices early in life to have a good long-term financial outcome is a myth. Economic growth is good for stock returns. The news media love to highlight economic data and investors love to follow it. The stock market prices forward-looking expectations. Stock prices represent expected future cash flows generated by real businesses. By the time you're reading about economic news or hearing about the growth potential of a market, it's highly likely already reflected in high stock prices. Dividends explain 40% of the stock market's historical returns. You're far more likely to pick a losing stock than a winning one, and missing the big winners makes it very difficult to match the return of the overall market, let alone beat it by selecting a subset of stocks. The other issue here is fees. The average index fund fee is a fraction of the average actively managed fund fee, which shifts the whole distribution of expected fund returns in favor of index funds. When we look at the data, this is exactly what we see. The vast majority of actively managed mutual funds underperform indexes and index funds by a wide margin. The asset-weighted average actively managed US equity mutual fund in the United States returned an annualized 9.41% for the 20 years ending December 2025, trailing a US equity index ETF by well over one percentage point. They give you top quartile returns, and they do it without taking on the risk of future underperformance. There has been an ontological debate about what money is going back thousands of years, and it really comes down to who should control money. If money is a thing that rises out of the free market as the most convenient intermediate good in trade or the best medium of exchange, then it is intrinsically valuable, and it should be left to the free market to control its supply and assign its value. This is, roughly speaking, the commodity theory of money. On the other hand, if money is an abstract idea based on mutual trust that ultimately relies on an authority to mediate it, the state should almost definitionally play an important role in how it's created and distributed. The authors explain that a leveraged life cycle strategy, meaning a strategy that starts with a leveraged stock allocation and gradually decreases leverage to ultimately become unleveraged near retirement, produces better retirement outcomes. The author's argument is basically that if you have a few thousand or even hundred thousand dollars to invest today, but your expected lifetime wealth is in the millions of dollars, you should be borrowing money to get as close as possible to having your intended lifetime allocation to stocks invested as soon as possible. Take what you spend in a year and multiply it by 25. Spend 40,000 a year, you need roughly a million. Spend 80,000, two million. And that 's the part nobody likes. Every time your lifestyle gets more expensive, your retirement gets further away. A bigger house, a nicer car, a subscription you forgot about. Each one isn't just a monthly cost. It's 25 times that added to the finish line. It also works in reverse. Cut $10,000 of yearly spending and you've just reduced the amount you need by a quarter of a million. It's not a perfect rule. Market crashes, inflation, and living to 100 can all mess with it. But it's a far better starting point than guessing. So the real question isn't how much you need to retire. It's how much your lifestyle is costing your future self. How war makes countries richer? People love saying World War II ended the Great Depression. Factories roared back to life. Unemployment basically disappeared, so war must be good for the economy, right? Here's the truth. War can make a country's numbers look incredible. That's not the same as making its people richer. When a country goes to war, the government spends enormous amounts of money, weapons, vehicles, uniforms, food, soldiers' wages. Factories run around the clock. Anyone who can work gets hired. In America during the war, unemployment fell to around 1%. But look at what those factories were making. Tanks, bombs, and planes that were built to be destroyed. Meanwhile, regular people faced rationing. Sugar, petrol, meat, tires, all limited. Everyone had a job and there was nothing to buy. Economists have a name for this mistake, the broken window fallacy. If someone smashes a shop window, the glazier gets paid and it looks like the economy grew. But the shop owner just spent money fixing a window instead of buying something new. Nothing was gained. Something was replaced. Now, some countries genuinely do profit. Weapons exporters, nations that supply both sides, and countries far from the actual fighting. And after a war, there's money to be made rebuilding everything that got flattened. But that money is coming from somewhere, usually from debt, destroyed cities, and people who didn't survive to see the recovery. War doesn't create wealth. It just moves it and sends the bill to the next generation. Why is saving actually a bad idea? You've been told your whole life to save money, put it away, don't touch it, be responsible. But here's the truth. If saving means letting cash sit in an account doing nothing, you're slowly losing money. The reason is inflation. Every year, prices go up, your groceries, your rent, your coffee. So the same $100 buys a little less every year. Let's say inflation runs at 3%. That doesn't sound like much, but at that rate, the buying power of your money halves in about 24 years. You didn't spend a cent. Half of it disappeared anyway. That's the trap. Your bank balance looks exactly the same. The number never drops, so it feels safe. But a number staying still while everything around it goes up isn't safety. It's a slow leap. Meanwhile, money that's invested can grow faster than inflation. Not every year and not without risk, but over long periods, it's historically outpaced cash sitting in a savings account by a huge margin. Now, this doesn't mean saving is useless. You absolutely need an emergency fund, cash you can grab instantly when your car breaks down or you lose your job. That money isn't meant to grow. It's meant to stop a bad week from becoming a financial disaster. The problem is when all your money lives there forever. So saving isn't bad. Saving is the first step. Stopping at saving is where it goes wrong. Why does a sale make you spend more? You walk into a store to grab one thing. Then you see a sign that says 40% off. 20 minutes later, you're walking out with three bags and a strange sense of accomplishment. You saved money, right? No, you spent it. Here's the thing. Your brain doesn't judge prices on their own. It judges them against a reference point. Stores show you the original price first, then cross it out. That crossed out number becomes the anchor. Suddenly, $60 doesn't feel like $60. It feels like $40 saved. And sometimes that original price was never real. Some retailers inflate it just so the discount looks bigger. Then they add pressure. Today only. Limited stock. Ends at midnight. Your brain hates losing out more than it loves gaining something. So the idea of missing a deal feels genuinely painful. Here's how powerful this is. In 2012, the department store JCPenney decided to get rid of sales and just offer honest, everyday prices. Customers hated it. Revenue dropped by about a quarter in a single year. The CEO was gone shortly after. Same products, same prices or lower. People just stopped buying because there was no deal to win. So a sale doesn't make things cheaper. It makes spending feel like winning. And if you only bought it because it was on sale, you didn't save 40%. You spent 60. What happens when a country goes bankrupt? When a person goes bankrupt, the bank takes their stuff. So when a whole country runs out of money, does someone come and repossess it? Not exactly. And what actually happens might be worse. Here's the thing. A country can't be shut down and sold off like a business. Nobody can foreclose on Argentina. So instead, when a country can't pay its debts, it defaults. It simply stops paying. Sounds like a great deal. It isn't. Once a country defaults, lenders stop trusting it. Borrowing becomes extremely expensive or impossible. Foreign investors pull their money out, the currency crashes, imports like fuel, food, and medicine suddenly cost a fortune. In 2022, Sri Lanka defaulted for the first time in its history. People queued for days for fuel. Hospitals ran short on medicine. The president fled the country. Some countries try to fix it by printing money. That's how Zimbabwe ended up printing a $100 trillion note that couldn't buy a loaf of bread. Others turned to the International Monetary Fund for a bailout. The catch is the conditions, spending cuts, higher taxes, reduced pensions. The country survives, but its people pay for it, often for years. And it can happen more than once. Argentina has defaulted on its debt nine times. So a country doesn't go bankrupt the way a person does. The government keeps running. It's the citizens who quietly go broke instead. Why is education so expensive now? Your parents could pay for university with a part-time job. Now a degree can cost more than a house deposit, and you'll be paying it off well into your 40s. So what changed? Did professors get that much better? Here's the truth. In many countries, especially the US, tuition has risen several times faster than inflation for decades. And a big reason is the money meant to make it affordable. When governments made student loans easy to get, universities suddenly had customers who could pay almost anything. So prices went up. More loans followed. Then prices went up again. Some economists argued the help became the problem. At the same time, a lot of public funding dried up. Governments started paying less per student, and universities filled the gap by charging students more. Then there's where the money goes. A growing chunk funds administration rather than teaching. Plus fancy gyms, new buildings and climbing walls, all designed to attract students in a competition for rankings. And universities have one huge advantage. Employers started demanding degrees for jobs that never needed them before. When a product becomes a requirement, the seller can charge whatever they want. That's the trap. You're not just buying an education, you're buying permission to apply for a job. And they know you'll pay. So education didn't get more valuable, it just got harder to avoid. Why is your data worth money if you're broke? You've got $19 in your account and a car that makes a concerning noise. So why are billion dollar companies so desperate to know what you're doing online? Here's the thing. They don't want your money, they want your attention and the ability to predict what you'll do next. Every like, search, scroll, pause, and location ping gets recorded. On their own, they're meaningless. Combined with millions of other people, they turn into a frighteningly accurate profile of what you want, what you fear, and what you'll click on. That profile gets sold to advertisers. Meta makes a couple of hundred dollars a year from each user in the US and Canada. You never paid a cent. You are the thing being sold. And being broke doesn't make you worthless, it makes you a specific kind of target. That's the dark part. Payday lenders, gambling apps, and buy now pay later services actively look for people under financial pressure. Struggling with money isn't a reason to stop showing you ads. For some companies, it's exactly the reason to start. Your data also has value beyond today. You might be broke now, but you'll buy a car, a phone, a home. Companies want to know you before you get there. So if you've ever wondered why the app is free, now you know. You're not the customer, you're the inventory. What's the difference between gross and net profit? A friend tells you their business made $100,000 last year. Sounds impressive until you find out they actually took home about 12. So where did the rest go? That's the the between gross and net. Let's say you run a coffee shop. You sell a coffee for $5. The beans, milk, and cup cost you $1.50. What's left is $3.50. That's your gross profit. It's what you make after paying for the product itself. Sounds great, but you're not done paying. There's rent, staff wages, electricity, insurance, marketing, equipment repairs, loan repayments, and eventually tax. Once every one of those costs is subtracted, whatever's left is your net profit. That's the money the business actually keeps. Here's the thing. Gross profit tells you if your product makes sense. Net profit tells you if your business makes sense. Plenty of businesses have amazing gross profit and terrible net profit. The coffee sells, the margins are healthy, but the rent is crushing them and the owner hasn't paid themselves in months. That's the trap. Gross profit is the number people brag about. Net profit is the number that decides whether you survive. So next time someone tells you how much their business makes, ask one question. Before or after everything else? What is cash flow? Here's something that sounds impossible. A business can be profitable and still go bankrupt. How? Cash flow. Cash flow is simply the movement of money in and out over time. Not how much you earn on paper, how much is actually in the bank right now and when it arrives versus when it leaves. Let's say you land a huge job, $50,000. Incredible. But the client pays in 90 days. Meanwhile, you need to buy materials, pay your staff, and cover rent all this month. On paper, you're profitable. In reality, you're broke for three months. And if you can't cover those bills in the meantime, the business dies before the money ever shows up. That's the trap. Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact. It works the same way in your personal life. You might earn a good salary, but if rent, car payments, and subscriptions all leave your account on the first and your pay arrives on the 15th, you'll spend two weeks every month feeling poor. So how do you deal with it? Know exactly when money comes in and when it goes out. Keep a buffer for the gaps and never money is real until it's actually sitting in your account. Because the bills don' care how much you're owed, they only care what you can pay today. What actually is financial freedom? Most people picture financial freedom as being rich, private jets, a mansion, never checking a price tag again. But here's the truth. Financial freedom has almost nothing to do with being rich. It means your money covers your life without you having to trade your time for it. That's it. Your investments, businesses, or assets bring in enough to pay for the way you live. Whether you keep working becomes a choice, not a requirement. And that's why the number is completely different for everyone. Someone spending 300,000 a year might be further from freedom than someone spending 30. A doctor earning a fortune with a huge mortgage and two car loans can be one bad month from disaster. A plumber with no debt and modest habits can be closer to free than they realize. Here's the thing. High income doesn't buy freedom. Low expenses do. Every dollar you don't need is a dollar you never have to earn again. And you don't have to get all the way there to feel the difference. Six months of savings means you can leave a terrible job. No debt means a bad year doesn't ruin you. Each step buys you a bit more choice. That's what financial freedom really is. Not the yacht, not the watch. It's the ability to say no and not worry about what it costs.
