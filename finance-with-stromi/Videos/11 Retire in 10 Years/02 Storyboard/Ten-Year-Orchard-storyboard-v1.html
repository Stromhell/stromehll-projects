The Ten-Year Orchard

An original Stromi script for Finance video 11. Your transcript was research only: story, cast, skits and every line are new, and all the YouTube-channel and Telegram promotion is gone. Nadia, a 32-year-old diner line cook, decides at 4 a.m. she wants out in ten years. A 1930s game show, THE NUMBER, runs the guess-the-number hook and the reveal. Her money is one apple tree in an orchard; three crows (Inflation, Taxes, Fees) peck the fruit; three kinds of soil are the scenarios; three levers (spend, time, earn) shrink the bill; a payday sprinkler is automation. Skit lines in red sit between narration like Two Thursdays and Frozen Market. Circular ending: ten years later a new cook asks Nadia the same question. Every number recomputed: the honest answer for a $3,000/month life is about $5,200/month, not the $3,000-4,000 the source claimed. Kept distinct from video 10 (no repeat of cushion, index-fund or crash steps; R41 points back to it).

2. The Twenty-Five Times Rule

R10The orchard (new)1:57

Next door, an old orchard at dusk. Barb, seventies, overalls, sits on a crate under a huge apple tree, peeling an apple with a pocket knife. Nadia leans on the fence.

R111994 planner office (new)2:08

Flashback, sepia tint, 1994. A planner's office with a boxy computer and stacks of paper. A pencil character runs down a giant scroll of years from 1926 onward, testing each one. Year cards flip past: 1929, 1937, 1966, 1973.

Narrator In 1994 a financial planner in California named William Bengen asked exactly that. He took real market history, back to 1926, and tested every thirty-year retirement he could. What's the most you can take out in year one, then raise it with inflation every year after, and never run out?

R121994 planner office (new)2:28

The pencil stops on 1966 and the scroll turns stormy grey. A thermometer gauge labelled WORST START drops to just above 4%. A rubber stamp slams: 4% RULE.

Narrator Even the worst start in his data, retiring in the mid-sixties right before years of high inflation and weak markets, survived thirty years at about four percent. A few years later, three professors at Trinity University ran a similar test and found much the same. That's where the famous four percent rule comes from.

R13The orchard (new)2:49

Barb carves "x 25" into the fence post with her knife. Floating numbers stack beside it: $36,000 x 25 = $900,000.

Narrator Four percent is one twenty-fifth. So the quick rule is: multiply your yearly spending by twenty-five. For Nadia, thirty-six thousand times twenty-five is nine hundred thousand dollars. That's the size of the orchard.

Nadia Nine hundred thousand?

Barb You asked how many apples. I didn't say it was a small tree.

R14The orchard (new)3:09

The fence post's x25 gets a second line scratched under it: x28 to x30, with a little tag "LONG RETIREMENT". Barb taps it with the knife handle.

Narrator And there's a catch nobody puts in the thumbnail. Bengen's test was built for thirty-year retirements. Nadia wants to stop at forty-two. Her money may need to last fifty years. For a retirement that long, many planners use a lower rate, closer to three and a half percent, which means something like twenty-eight times spending instead of twenty-five. We'll use nine hundred thousand to keep the math clean, but keep that cushion in mind.

Barb I retired at fifty. I still only eat three and a half apples.

4. The Three Crows

Chapter card, 3 s, piano sting + whoosh.

R20The orchard (new)4:35

A shadow passes. Three scruffy crows land on the branches, each wearing a little name tag: INFLATION, TAXES, FEES. They start pecking chunks out of the fruit.

Narrator Now, here's where a lot of retirement videos lie to you by accident. They show a chart growing at seven or ten percent and stop. But every orchard has crows.

Crow (Inflation) Morning!

Crow (Taxes) Don't mind us.

Crow (Fees) We're only taking a little.

R21The orchard (new)4:50

Crow Inflation pecks; a price tag on a loaf of bread swells. A pile of fruit marked $173,000 shrinks to a smaller pile marked $129,000 IN TODAY'S MONEY.

Narrator The first crow is inflation. Say you invest a thousand dollars a month for ten years and it grows seven percent a year. You'd have about a hundred and seventy-three thousand. Sounds great. But if prices rise three percent a year, that pile buys only what about a hundred and twenty-nine thousand buys today. You put in a hundred and twenty thousand. So most of the growth was just keeping up.

Crow (Inflation) I don't eat the apple. I just make the apple smaller.

R22The orchard (new)5:22

A big chalkboard nailed to the barn: 8% advertised, minus 3% inflation, minus 1% taxes, minus 0.5% fees = 3.5% REAL. Each crow rubs out its slice with a wing.

Narrator Put all three crows together. A portfolio that earns eight percent on paper. Inflation takes three. Taxes take about one. Fees take half a percent. What actually grows your buying power is about three and a half. That's called your real return, and it's the only one you can retire on.

R23The orchard (new)5:42

Crow Fees tries to perch on a tiny fence sign reading INDEX FUND 0.05%. The sign is too small; it falls off. Crow Taxes gets shut out by a little wire cage labelled TAX-SHELTERED ACCOUNT.

Narrator The good news? You can shoo two of the crows. Fees are the easiest. On ten years of Nadia's investing, just one percent a year in fees costs her nearly fifty thousand dollars. A broad low-cost index fund can charge a tiny fraction of that. And taxes shrink a lot inside tax-sheltered retirement accounts, like a 401(k) or an IRA in the US.

Crow (Fees) Hey! I had a family to feed!

Nadia Not off my tree.

R24The orchard (new)6:10

Barb hangs a lantern labelled EVERYTHING IN TODAY'S DOLLARS on the tree. The crow Inflation stays put, grumbling, but now every fruit has a price tag in today's money.

Narrator Inflation, you can't shoo. So from here on, we'll do the smart thing: count everything in today's dollars, and use returns after inflation. That way nine hundred thousand means nine hundred thousand of today's groceries.

Crow (Inflation) Rude. Accurate. But rude.
