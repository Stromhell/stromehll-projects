## CHAPTER 1: THE EIGHT-YEAR CAKE (0:45-2:45)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: Grand Theft Auto 6 is made by Rockstar Games, and Rockstar is owned by a company called Take-Two Interactive. Take-Two has never said what the game cost. But we can watch the cost pile up, because it leaves a mark on the books.
SKIT (a bakery. GUS is icing a wedding cake so tall it disappears into the ceiling. DOT taps the counter.)
DOT: Is it ready?
GUS: It's being polished.
DOT: You said that in 2023.
GUS: And it was being polished then, too.

NARRATOR: Here's how companies treat a cake like that. While a game is still in the oven, the money spent on it isn't counted as a loss straight away. It sits on the balance sheet as an asset, a kind of "this will pay us back later" pile. Take-Two calls it capitalized software development costs.
VISUAL: A glass jar on the bakery shelf labelled "STILL IN THE OVEN", filling with coins year by year. Year tags pop on the glass.
NARRATOR: In 2020 that pile was about four hundred and forty million dollars. Then five hundred million. Eight hundred million. Over a billion. One and a half. Nearly two. And by March this year, about two point three five billion dollars, sitting in the jar, waiting for one game to ship.
NARRATOR: Not all of that is one game, but the biggest slice almost certainly is. Outside estimates put the full bill for GTA 6 somewhere between one and two billion dollars, and for comparison, GTA 5 in 2013 was estimated at around two hundred and sixty-five million, including marketing.
SKIT (Gus, sweating, slices a tiny sliver off the giant cake and hands it to Dot.)
GUS: That'll be eighty dollars.
DOT: For a slice?
GUS: For eight years.

NARRATOR: Thousands of people, a dozen studios, almost eight years of full production. And it only pays back if people buy the slice.

## CHAPTER 2: THE GOOSE THAT WOULDN'T STOP (2:45-4:50)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: To understand why Take-Two was willing to bake for eight years, you have to understand the last cake.
VISUAL: A farm at sunset. WENDELL, in a farmer's hat over his usual top hat, holds a goose. The goose lays a golden egg. Then another.
NARRATOR: GTA 5 came out in September 2013. It passed a billion dollars in sales in three days, faster than any entertainment product before it. And then it simply refused to stop. It's been sold again on three generations of consoles. Take-Two now counts well over two hundred million copies, and the online side, with its in-game money and a monthly subscription, has kept the eggs coming for thirteen years.
SKIT (Wendell sits on a pile of golden eggs, reading the newspaper.)
WENDELL: Thirteen years. Same goose.
DOT (off-screen): Ever think about getting a second goose?
WENDELL: Oh, I did. I bought a whole farm of them.

NARRATOR: That's the other half of the story. In 2022, Take-Two bought the mobile games company Zynga, in a deal valued at about twelve point seven billion dollars when it was announced. On the day it was announced, Take-Two's stock fell about thirteen percent.
VISUAL: Wendell opens a barn. Hundreds of little phone-shaped chickens. None of them lay anything.
NARRATOR: Then came the write-downs. When a company admits it overpaid for something, it has to shrink that number on its books, and the shrink shows up as a loss. Take-Two wrote down about two point three billion in one year and about three and a half billion the next. Add it up with everything else, and the company lost roughly eight and a half billion dollars over three years.
SKIT (Wendell, holding one silent phone-chicken, looks at the camera.)
WENDELL: So, about that second goose.
(The original golden goose lays one more egg, loudly.)

NARRATOR: Here's the uncomfortable bit. Back in 2014, GTA was about sixty-nine percent of Take-Two's revenue. Today it's a much smaller slice of a much bigger, loss-making company. So the company didn't just need a hit. It needed the old goose to lay the biggest egg in history.

## CHAPTER 4: THE $8 BILLION PROMISE (6:20-8:10)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: Here's the promise Take-Two made to its investors. This fiscal year, which runs to the end of March, it expects eight to eight point two billion dollars in net bookings. Net bookings is basically the money customers commit to spend, whether or not the game has shipped. Last year it was six point seven two billion.
VISUAL: A giant tightrope between two skyscrapers. One end says "6.72", the other "8.0-8.2". A tiny cardboard box marked "VI" balances in the middle.
NARRATOR: And the first half of the year didn't help. In the quarter to September, net bookings fell eighteen percent, to about one point six billion. So almost all of that jump has to land in the six months from October to March. Roughly one point seven billion dollars more than the same months last year, mostly riding on one game.
NARRATOR: On the ninth of November, ten days before launch, Take-Two held its line. It kept the eight billion forecast, and the boss said pre-orders were running far ahead of what the company expected. The stock went up.
SKIT (game store, midnight queue outside. PIP behind the counter. A customer, DEV, holds two boxes: one says "$80", one says "$100, ULTIMATE".)
PIP: Standard or ultimate?
DEV: What's the difference?
PIP: Twenty dollars.
DEV: ...Obviously ultimate.

NARRATOR: Dev is not alone. By mid-October, the tracking firm Sensor Tower estimated that close to ninety percent of early digital buyers had picked the hundred-dollar edition, when deluxe editions normally take maybe one in five sales. By the eleventh of November, it estimated around ten million digital pre-orders, worth about a billion dollars, before a single person had played it.

## CHAPTER 5: THE $80 GAME IS CHEAP. THE REST ISN'T. (8:10-9:50)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: Eighty dollars for a video game sounds outrageous. But here's a fun bit of maths. In 2013, a big new console game cost sixty dollars. Run that through the government's own inflation numbers and today it's roughly eighty-six. So in real money, the eighty-dollar game is actually a little cheaper than GTA 5 was.
SKIT (Dev and DOT at their kitchen table. Dot has a calculator.)
DOT: The game's fine. Now the console.
DEV: How much?
DOT: The PlayStation 5 launched at five hundred. Since April, six hundred and fifty. The big Xbox launched at five hundred too. Since August, eight hundred.
DEV (very quietly): ...I'll just play it at your cousin's house.

NARRATOR: That's the strange part of this boom. Console makers have historically sold the box at a loss and made it back on games. Now the box itself has become the expensive part, and fewer people are buying one. In August, Americans bought fewer pieces of gaming hardware than in any August since 2013, while the average price paid hit a record.
NARRATOR: So the real question for Take-Two was never "will gamers pay eighty dollars". It was "how many people already own the box, and will the rest pay six or eight hundred for the right to pay eighty more".

## CHAPTER 6: THE BILLION-DOLLAR SICK DAY (9:50-12:00)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: Now, that famous billion dollars.

NARRATOR: An economist in Barcelona, José García Montalvo, put a number on launch day: around one and a half million American workers calling in, at an average output of roughly seventy thousand dollars a year each, for around a billion dollars of lost work. It went everywhere.
VISUAL: Second quote card: "I think a lot of people will be calling in sick on November 19th." Strauss Zelnick, CEO, Take-Two (as reported).
NARRATOR: Even Take-Two's own chief executive joked about it. And the calendar didn't help: the game came out exactly one week before Thanksgiving. One sick Thursday, one "while I'm at it" Friday, and a four-day weekend waiting on the other side of the turkey.

RITA: Okay. Agenda item one. Where is everyone?
INTERN: Dev has the flu.
RITA: And Marco?
INTERN: Same flu.
RITA: And Jess?

RITA (long pause): Jess lives in Ohio.

NARRATOR: But let's be honest about that billion. First, it's a back-of-the-envelope estimate, not a published study, and even the one and a half million figure is shaky. Second, the US economy produces something like a hundred and twenty billion dollars of stuff on an average working day. A billion is less than one percent of one day. And third, the money doesn't vanish. The people who stayed home didn't burn their wages. They spent eighty or a hundred dollars on a game, plus pizza, plus energy drinks.
VISUAL: A seesaw. Left seat "LOST WORK: ~$1B". Right seat fills up with a game box, pizza boxes, cans. It levels out.
NARRATOR: So the honest version is: the economy didn't lose a billion dollars. It moved some of it from offices to living rooms, and from bosses to one game company. Which is exactly the point. Every one of those sick days ends up somewhere. And a lot of them end up in Take-Two's bank account.

## YOUR WALLET: THREE LESSONS (13:40-15:00)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: You don't need to own a single game share for this story to matter to your money. Three things to take from it.
VISUAL: Corkboard, three index cards pinned one by one, each with a doodle.
NARRATOR: One. Check how much of your money rides on one thing. Priya's shares lost close to a tenth in a day because of one date. If one company, one product or one stock is a big part of what you own, a single headline can move your whole net worth. Index funds spread that risk across hundreds of companies. That's not a tip, it's just how risk works.
NARRATOR: Two. The good news is usually already in the price. By launch day, everyone knew this would be huge. Markets try to price in what everyone already knows, which is why "the biggest launch ever" and "the stock went up" are not the same sentence.
NARRATOR: Three. Watch the twenty-dollar nudge. Nine in ten early buyers chose the more expensive edition. That's not a crime, it's a great product. But the upgrade button works on everything, from phones to flights to fries. Decide your fun budget before the shop decides it for you.
SKIT (Dev, at the store, hand hovering over "ULTIMATE". DOT just raises one eyebrow. Dev picks it anyway.)
DEV: It's a fun budget. This is fun.
DOT: That was the whole fun budget.
DEV: ...For the year?

NARRATOR: And a bonus fourth one, for Dev: a sick day you're not sick for isn't free. Somebody pays for it. Usually it's your paid time off, your reputation, or Rita.
