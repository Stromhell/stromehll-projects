The IOU Auction

An original Stromi script for Finance video 12, "What Happens If Everyone Stops Buying US Debt?" (your exact title). The transcript was research only: story, cast, skits and every line are new, and the source host's name and calls to action are gone. Rosa and Teo have lost sixteen offers on a house. Their problem starts in a 1930s auction hall where Mister Tab (the US government) sells IOUs every week. Four giant dominoes (Auction, Interest, Mortgage, Breakage) fall one per chapter, and the last lands on their house. Skit lines in red sit between narration like Two Thursdays and Frozen Market. A balanced "will everyone really stop?" chapter keeps it a risk, not a prophecy. Circular ending: offer #17, and Mister Tab asking the room again. Kept apart from video 09 (yen carry trade, Japan's intervention, dollar and gold reserve shares): one line plus an end card pointing to it.

Cast (all made up, all new)

  • Rosa and Teo: young couple, 16 lost offers
  • Mister Tab: the borrower, top hat, sweats a lot
  • Madame Reserve: bidder for the world's central banks, packing for the gold shop
  • Ferdie: the Fed's bidder, hand-cranked printing press
  • Flash: hedge-fund kid, three phones, bids with borrowed cash
  • Del: dealer bank, must bid at every auction
  • Auctioneer, a neighbour under a "locked under 4%" umbrella, Pip, Dot, Stromi mascot in Your Wallet

1. America Runs a Tab

D06Mister Tab's mansion of boxes (new)1:01

Mister Tab at home: a mansion full of boxes. A payslip pops up beside him: IN $50,000. A receipt unrolls across the floor: OUT $60,000. A red gap of $10,000 flashes between them.

Narrator Start with the simple bit. America spends more than it collects in taxes. Last year the gap was about two trillion dollars. Shrink that to a household and it's like earning fifty thousand dollars a year and spending sixty.

Mister Tab The other ten just... appears.

Narrator It doesn't appear. It gets borrowed.

D07IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)1:20

Close on one paper IOU in Mister Tab's hand. It animates like a coupon book: LEND ME $1,000. I PAY YOU $30 A YEAR. IN 10 YEARS YOU GET YOUR $1,000 BACK. A rubber stamp slams: US TREASURY.

Narrator A country can't swipe a credit card, so it sells IOUs. Officially they're called Treasury bonds, but an IOU is all they are. You hand over a thousand dollars. You get interest every year. At the end you get your thousand back.

Narrator And the interest rate on that IOU has a fancy name too. The yield. Same thing. Remember that word, because it runs this whole story.

D08IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)1:46

Wide on the bidders. Labels pop over each row: MADAME RESERVE (other countries' central banks), FERDIE (the Fed), FLASH (hedge funds), DEL (dealer banks), plus pension funds and regular savers at the back.

Narrator For years, two bidders in this room were the most reliable lenders on Earth. Foreign central banks, who keep their emergency savings in dollars. And the Federal Reserve, America's own central bank. Neither of them haggled much.

Madame Reserve Darling, I'll take a billion. I don't need the receipt.

Ferdie I'll take whatever's left. I brought my own printer.

D09IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)2:08

Ferdie at the back feeds blank paper into a little hand-cranked printing press beside his seat. Fresh dollars flutter into his paddle. Pip leans over to watch, horrified.

Narrator Wait. The Fed prints money to buy the government's IOUs? Yes. After two thousand eight, and again in twenty twenty, it created trillions of dollars and bought Treasury bonds with them. America, in a sense, lent money to America.

Pip That's allowed?

Ferdie If you do it in a suit, yes.

Narrator One of the big reasons happened in twenty twenty-two. After Russia invaded Ukraine, America and Europe froze roughly three hundred billion dollars of Russia's central bank reserves. Whatever you think about that war, every central banker on Earth learned the same lesson that week.

Madame Reserve If they can lock his savings, they can lock mine.

D13IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)3:10

A big map of China lights up on a screen. A bar chart drains: 2013 $1.3 TRILLION -> 2026 $618 BILLION. Beside it, a gold bar thumps onto a scale.

Narrator China was America's biggest foreign lender in twenty thirteen, with about one point three trillion dollars of Treasuries. In the latest official figures, it's six hundred and eighteen billion. The lowest since two thousand eight. A gold bar can't be frozen by anyone else's government.

Narrator Japan has been selling too, partly to defend the yen. That's a whole story of its own, and we told it in our video on the carry trade. Link at the end.

D14IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)3:40

Ferdie stops cranking his printer, pulls a dust sheet over it, and only raises his paddle for tiny short-dated IOUs marked 3 MONTHS. The long 10-YEAR and 30-YEAR stacks pile up unsold in front of the podium.

Narrator And Ferdie? The Fed spent years shrinking its pile of bonds, because printing money to buy them helps push up inflation. These days, when it does buy, it's mostly short-term bills that get paid back within a year. Not the long ten- and thirty-year bonds that matter most for a mortgage.

Mister Tab Ferdie, buddy! The long ones!

Ferdie My doctor says no long ones.

D15IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)4:04

The empty velvet seats fill with new bidders: pension funds, bond funds and Flash, a kid with slicked hair, three phones and a paddle taped to a bank loan form. Flash bounces his knee non-stop.

Narrator Someone did fill those seats. Pension funds, bond funds, banks, and hedge funds like Flash. But they're a very different kind of lender. They don't have to buy. They buy when the price is right. And some of them buy with borrowed money, which means when markets get scary, they don't wait. They sell.

Flash I love you, Mister Tab. I love you so much. What's your rate? Is that your final rate? I'm leaving.

3. Domino One: The Auction

D17IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)4:36

The Auctioneer bangs the gavel. Paddles go up with numbers on them: 4.9%, 5.0%, 5.1%, 5.3%. A board fills up from the cheapest offer, ticking up until a big bar labelled SOLD fills to the top.

Narrator Here's how the auction actually works. Lenders don't bid a price for a painting. They bid the interest they want. The government fills the order from the cheapest bids first and works up until it's raised what it needs. Lots of eager lenders, cheap money. Fewer eager lenders, expensive money.

Auctioneer Do I hear five? Five-one? Five-three to the gentleman with three phones!

D18IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)5:00

A conveyor belt behind the podium carries IOUs out of a door marked DUE TODAY and straight back in through a door marked NEW. Mister Tab hands old IOUs back with one hand and sells new ones with the other.

Narrator And the auctions never stop. In the last full budget year, the Treasury ran four hundred and forty-four of them. It borrowed about one point nine trillion of new money and rolled over another nine trillion of old debt that came due. How do you pay back an old IOU? You sell a new one.

Dot That's paying one credit card with another credit card.

Mister Tab Forever. Yes. Isn't it elegant?

D19IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)5:27

Del, a tired dealer-bank clerk in sleeve garters, sits in the front row chained by the ankle to his chair with a tag: MUST BID. He raises his paddle every single time without looking up from his newspaper.

Narrator So could an auction just fail, with nobody buying at all? In practice, no. A group of big banks called primary dealers are expected to bid at every auction. No modern Treasury auction has gone unsold. That sounds reassuring. It isn't the point. The point is the price.

Del I always buy. I just don't always buy cheap.

D20IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)5:49

Newspaper headline spins in: OCT 7, 2026. $39 BILLION 10-YEAR NOTES. 5.30%. HIGHEST SINCE 2000. Mister Tab mops his forehead with a handkerchief that is itself an IOU.

Narrator And the price is climbing. On October seventh, twenty twenty-six, the government sold thirty-nine billion dollars of ten-year IOUs at five point three percent. The highest rate at a ten-year auction since two thousand. The buyers still turned up. They just charged more.

Mister Tab Five point three. On a Tuesday. Without even dinner first.

D21IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)6:10

Flashback card, April 2025. Flash panics, sprays IOUs into the air like a card dealer. A yield gauge on the wall swings into the red. In a little TV in the corner, a president holds a hand to his ear, watching the bond screen.

Narrator And the new lenders are jumpy. In April twenty twenty-five, the week big tariffs were announced, investors dumped Treasuries and yields jumped. Within days most of the tariffs were paused. Asked about it, the president said the bond market was very tricky, and that he'd been watching it.

Flash I was not panicking. I was cardio-selling.

Narrator The most powerful man in the world, watching his lenders. That's the first domino.

D22IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)6:37

The first giant domino, 1 AUCTION, finally tips. It falls in slow motion with a deep wooden CLACK onto domino 2, INTEREST, which begins to rock.

Narrator And when it tips, it lands on the government's own budget.

D26Mister Tab's mansion of boxes (new)7:26

D28Mister Tab's mansion of boxes (new)8:11

Narrator So how does a government pay a bill that size? Three doors. Raise taxes. Cut spending. Or the third door, the quiet one, that governments reach for when the first two hurt too much. Hold that thought. We'll open it soon.

D36IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)10:15

A side market behind the auction hall: a busy flea-market stall called USED IOUs. Pip holds up an old IOU paying $30 a year. Next to him, a shiny new one pays $50. Shoppers walk straight past Pip to the new one.

Narrator Remember, you don't have to keep an IOU for ten years. You can sell it to someone else. But the price isn't fixed. Say Pip bought an old one that pays thirty dollars a year. Now new ones pay fifty. Who gives Pip a full thousand dollars for his? Nobody.

Pip Eight hundred and forty-six?

Narrator That's roughly the fair price. When rates rise, old bonds lose value. Everyone holding them is sitting on a loss.

D37Small-town bank, rate staircase (new)10:43

Inside the bank vault, shelves of old IOUs with red price tags. A big tag swings down: UNREALISED LOSSES, US BANKS: $327 BILLION. The monocled loan officer polishes his monocle nervously.

Narrator Banks, pension funds and bond funds hold a lot of old IOUs. American banks alone were sitting on about three hundred and twenty-seven billion dollars of paper losses in the middle of this year. Paper losses only become real if you're forced to sell. In twenty twenty-three, Silicon Valley Bank was forced to sell. It lasted about forty-eight hours.

Del Paper losses are fine. Until you need the paper.

D38IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)11:10

Flashback card, March 2020. Masks, panic. Everyone in the auction hall rushes the exit waving IOUs: Madame Reserve, Flash, the pension funds. A yield gauge on the wall spins wildly. IOUs fly like pigeons.

Narrator We've already seen the rush for the exit once. March twenty twenty. Covid hit and everyone wanted cash at the same moment. Foreign investors alone sold about four hundred and seventeen billion dollars of Treasuries in that single month. Even the safest market on Earth started to jam.

Flash Everybody's selling, so I'm selling, so everybody's selling!

D39IOU auction hall + dominoes (new)11:32

Ferdie whips the dust sheet off his printing press and cranks it like a ship's wheel. A river of fresh dollars pours into the hall, buying every IOU on the floor. The yield gauge settles back down.

Narrator So the Fed opened the third door. It created money and bought roughly one point six trillion dollars of Treasuries in about three months. And it worked. Rates calmed down. That's how you rescue a bond market. You print money and lend it to yourself.

Ferdie Problem solved!

Narrator Problem moved.
