S06Bank waiting room (new)0:55

Mort the teller, small round glasses, leans over his counter and slides Wren a single penny on a velvet tray. A plaque behind him: STANDARD SAVINGS 0.01% APY.

Narrator Here's what nobody at the counter tells you. Many big banks still pay about one hundredth of one percent on a standard savings account. On ten thousand dollars, that's about one dollar a year. The national average across all banks is a bit better, under half a percent.

Mort Your yearly interest, madam.

Wren It's a penny on a cushion.

Mort It's a very nice cushion.

S07Bank waiting room (new)1:20

A shadowy figure in a long coat walks between the chairs: INFLATION, drawn as a tall thin man with scissors. He snips a sliver off each coin as he passes. The coins don't notice.

Narrator And there's a second visitor in the waiting room. Prices in America rose about three point seven percent over the last year. So every year your money sits still, it buys a little less. Your ten thousand doesn't shrink on the screen. It shrinks at the grocery store.

Dime Did somebody just give me a haircut?

Gus Every year, pal. Every year.

S08Wren's apartment (new)1:43

Wren's fridge door becomes a whiteboard. Two jars side by side drawn in marker: LEFT jar labelled 1% grows a tiny bit each year; RIGHT jar labelled 10% grows faster, then shoots upward. A year counter runs 1 to 20.

Narrator Now let's do the math with the same ten thousand dollars. At one percent a year, after twenty years you'd have about twelve thousand two hundred. Now imagine it earned what the US stock market has averaged over the last hundred years, around ten percent a year, with dividends reinvested. After twenty years? About sixty-seven thousand.

Narrator Same money. Same twenty years. The only thing that changed is where it sat.

S09Wren's apartment (new)2:11

Close on the right-hand jar. The coins inside start breeding little coins of their own, which then breed more. The jar overflows onto the counter. Wren catches one.

Narrator That bend at the end is compounding. Your money earns money, then that money earns money too. In the early years it looks like nothing. Later it does most of the work.

Wren Why didn't anybody tell me this?

Pip They did. You were in the waiting room. It's very quiet in there.

S10Market Street (new)2:31

A wide glide down Market Street: bakeries, phone makers, pharmacies, car plants, a sign shop. Coins in hard hats hurry in and out of doors. Over it all, a heads-up tag: AVERAGE, NOT A PROMISE.

Narrator One honest warning before we go further. Ten percent is a long-run average, not a yearly promise. In two thousand eight the market lost about thirty-seven percent. In twenty twenty-three it gained about twenty-six. Over decades it has trended up. Over any single year, it can do anything.

3. Plank One: The Cushion

Chapter card, 3 s, piano sting + whoosh.

S17Canyon rope bridge (new)4:06

Wren steps up to the bridge. The first plank is a big soft cushion tied into the rope. Dime sits on her shoulder. The canyon wind blows her hair.

Narrator Plank one. Before a single dollar crosses, you build a cushion. Three to six months of your real living costs, kept in cash. Rent, groceries, car, phone. Not a guess. Your actual number.

Wren My actual number is twenty-four hundred a month.

Dime So, about seven to fourteen thousand in the cushion.

Wren Seven thousand, two hundred, minimum. I'll go to nine.

S18Market Street (new)4:29

A flash-forward on Market Street: a storm rolls in, prices on the shopfronts drop. A worried investor coin, holding a car-repair bill, runs out of a shop selling everything at half price.

Narrator Why so much cash first? Because the worst time to sell an investment is when you have to. The car dies, the job goes, the market is down that same month, and you sell at the bottom to pay the bill. The cushion means life never gets to pick the day you sell.

S19Bank waiting room (new)4:50

Mort's counter. Wren pulls her cushion money out of the 0.01% account and walks it across the street to a brighter little bank with a sign: HIGH-YIELD SAVINGS 4.3%. Mort waves a tiny sad handkerchief.

Narrator And the cushion doesn't have to sit in the worst chair. Online high-yield savings accounts are paying around four to four and a half percent right now, with the same government deposit insurance up to two hundred fifty thousand dollars. On nine thousand, that's roughly three hundred and ninety dollars a year, instead of about ninety cents.

Mort You're leaving?

Wren Just the cushion. You can keep the penny.

4. Plank Two: Two Pockets

Chapter card, 3 s, piano sting + whoosh.

S21Wren's apartment (new)5:19

Wren at her kitchen table with the label maker. She labels two pockets sewn onto a denim apron: RAINY DAY and SOMEDAY. She drops coins into each. The SOMEDAY coins put on tiny hard hats.

Narrator Plank two is in your head. Your money now has two different jobs. The rainy-day pocket is the cushion. It never moves, it's always there, and it's boring on purpose. The someday pocket is money you won't need for at least five years. That money is allowed to go up and down, because its job is to grow.

Wren Rainy day. Someday.

Dime Which one am I?

Wren You've been waiting eleven years. You're someday.

S22Wren's apartment (new)5:47

Wren checks her investing app; a line wiggles down. Instead of panicking, she taps the RAINY DAY pocket and it shows a steady flat balance. She shrugs and goes back to her tea.

Narrator This sounds too simple to matter. It matters a lot. When the someday pocket drops, you look at the rainy-day pocket, see that rent is covered, and the drop becomes a number instead of an emergency.

Pip Market's down four percent.

Wren Is rent down four percent?

Pip No.

Wren Then I'm fine.

S30Market Street (new)7:49

Gus hands Wren a tiny paper deed. As she unfolds it, it stretches the whole length of Market Street, every shop stamped with a little slice: YOURS. A tag pops up: S&P 500 INDEX FUND, ABOUT 500 COMPANIES.

Narrator That's an index fund. Instead of betting on one shop, you buy a tiny slice of the whole street. An S&P 500 fund holds about five hundred of the biggest American companies. A total market fund holds thousands. If one shop closes, you barely feel it. If the street grows, you grow with it.

Narrator And the cheapest of them charge about three cents a year for every hundred dollars you put in. Three cents.

S31Market Street (new)8:18

A row of fancy fund managers in shiny suits race against a slow, plain turtle wearing an S&P 500 sash. The turtle crosses the finish line first. The managers check their watches.

Narrator Does it really beat the experts? Mostly, yes. S&P's own scorecard keeps finding that over fifteen years, around nine in ten US large-company funds run by professional pickers do worse than the plain index. They charge more and still lose to the turtle.

The fridge whiteboard again. Wren draws a staircase of three hundred dollar coins, one per month, climbing across 25 years. At the end, a giant tower: about $285,000. A small stack beside it: $90,000 put in.

Dime So I'm not a coin. I'm a parent.

S35Wren's apartment (new)9:31

A second, smaller tower appears next to the first, built by a cartoon Wren who waited. It's much shorter. A tag: START 10 YEARS LATER: ~$104,000.

Narrator Now the twist. Same three hundred a month, same eight percent, but she waits ten years before starting and invests for fifteen. She ends up with about a hundred and four thousand. Waiting a third of the time cost her nearly two thirds of the money. The early years are where the bend starts.

S36Market Street storm sale (new)9:52

A storm hits Market Street. Shop prices crash and the signs flip to HALF PRICE. Everyone runs out. Wren walks in with her usual $300 and leaves with an armful of shares.

Narrator And then, at some point, the storm. The market will crash. It always has, many times. The S&P lost more than half its value from twenty oh seven to twenty oh nine. In twenty twenty it fell about a third in one month. Here's what happens if you just keep walking.

S37Market Street storm sale (new)10:12

Three big price tags on a shopfront: MONTH 1 $100, MONTH 2 $50, MONTH 3 $100. Under each, Wren's $300 becomes 3 shares, then 6 shares, then 3 shares. A final tally: 12 shares x $100 = $1,200, for $900 put in.

Narrator Month one, shares cost a hundred dollars. Her three hundred buys three. Month two, the crash, shares cost fifty. Same three hundred buys six. Month three, back to a hundred, three more. She put in nine hundred dollars. The price ended exactly where it started. But she owns twelve shares, worth twelve hundred.

Rico How did you make money on a crash?

Wren I kept walking. You sold.

S38Canyon rope bridge (new)10:38

Wind howls through the canyon. Wren grips the ropes on plank five, but keeps stepping. Behind her, the waiting room window. Ahead, Market Street lights flicker back on one by one.

Narrator No promise here. Some recoveries took years, and after twenty-oh-nine it was about four years before the index got back to its old high. That's exactly why the cushion and the five-year rule come first. They're what let you keep walking when the bridge shakes.

S40Wren's apartment (new)10:59

Wren at the kitchen table with a fresh notebook. Stromi the mascot pops up from behind the label maker and taps each line as she writes it.

Narrator One. Find your real monthly number, and build three to six months of it as a cushion. Two. Move that cushion out of the penny chair into a high-yield, insured savings account. Three.
