S30Tabletop props7:50

Narrator They split the credit card into its two jobs. One job is paying. You buy something now without cash. The other job is lending. You carry a balance, and interest starts to run.

S31Tabletop props8:13

The PAYING shop: income jar (INTERCHANGE + ANNUAL FEES), expense jars (REWARDS, FRAUD, FLOAT). A clock shows the days between tap and due date.

Narrator That last one is easy to miss. When you tap, your bank pays for your groceries long before you pay your bank. In between, it's lending you that hundred dollars for free, as long as you pay in full.

S32Tabletop props8:40

Narrator So on its own, does the paying business make money? Here's what the Fed found. By the end of twenty twenty-one, after all the other costs of handling purchases, the banks were left with about a dollar thirty for every hundred dollars their customers spent.

Narrator Then came the rewards. About a dollar fifty. A dollar thirty in. A dollar fifty out. On its own, the paying business was slightly underwater.

Narrator How does a business end up paying out more than it takes in? It hadn't always been that way. At first, the purchase side still made a little money. Then the reward race heated up.

Narrator Between twenty fifteen and twenty twenty, rewards cost about a quarter more per dollar of purchases. Spending on rewards cards grew faster than spending on cards without them, and the margin on purchases slid below zero.

Narrator Across the whole period, the paying side added slightly less than nothing to the bank's card profits. About minus four percent.

Narrator And the reward rate kept inching up. On rewards cards, the CFPB puts it at about one point six percent of spending in twenty twenty-four, up from one point four in twenty twenty-one.
