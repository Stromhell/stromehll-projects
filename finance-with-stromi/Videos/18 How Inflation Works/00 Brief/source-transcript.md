Paul is cleaning out a kitchen drawer when he finds an old grocery receipt. It's from early 2020. A normal weekly shop. Bread, milk, eggs, coffee, chicken, a tank of gas on the way home. That evening, he buys the exact same things again. The total is about 30% higher. Same bread, same milk, same Paul. His wife Anne looks over his shoulder. We're not buying more, she says. We're just paying more. His paycheck went up, too. just not by that much. So, where did the money go? The answer is a word you hear on the news every month. Inflation. Let's take it apart. What it is, where it comes from, who it helps, who it hurts, and what it means for you. First, what is inflation exactly? Inflation is when prices across the whole economy rise over time. Not just one thing getting expensive, almost everything, a little at a time. Another way to say it, each dollar buys a little less than it did before. Over a long time, it adds up. Something that cost $1 in 1913 costs about $34 today. Your grandparents prices weren't cheap. Their dollars were just worth more. In America, the government measures it every month. Statisticians track the prices of a giant imaginary shopping basket. Food, rent, gas, cars, doctor visits, haircuts, tens of thousands of prices. That's the consumer price index. When people say inflation is 3%, they mean that basket costs 3% more than a year ago. And here's the part people miss. Inflation going down doesn't mean prices go down. It means they rise more slowly. Most central banks actually aim for a little inflation. The Federal Reserve's target is 2% a year. A little is seen as healthy. A lot is a problem. So where does it come from? There are three main engines. Engine one, too much money chasing too few goods. Imagine an auction. If everyone suddenly gets more money, but there's still only one painting, the price goes up. That's what happened after 2020. Governments sent out trillions in relief. Interest rates were near zero. The amount of money in the US economy grew by about 40% in roughly 2 years. Engine two. Something gets scarce or expensive to make. Factories closed. Ships got stuck. Chips ran out. Fewer goods, higher prices. Engine three, expectations. If everyone believes prices will rise, workers ask for bigger raises and stores raise prices in advance. And those beliefs can become self-fulfilling. Put all three together and in June 2022, US inflation hit 9.1%. The highest in 40 years. So, how do you cool it down? The Fed's main tool is interest rates. raise rates and borrowing gets expensive. Mortgages, car loans, credit cards, people and businesses spend less. Demand cools. Price increases slow down. From 2022 to 2023, the Fed raised rates faster than at any time in four decades. It worked slowly. By 2025, inflation was back under 3%. Then this year, a different engine kicked in. At the end of February, a war broke out involving Iran. Shipping through the straight of Hormuz, the route for a huge share of the world's oil was disrupted. Brent crude went from about $60 a barrel in January to over 118 by the end of March. Paul felt it first at the pump. By September, gasoline was up 34% from a year earlier. Overall inflation climbed back to 3.7%. But notice something. If you remove food and energy, so-called core inflation was only 2.6%. Economists watch core to see what's driven by shocks and what's spreading through the whole economy. An oil shock is like a fever. The question is whether it becomes an infection. If oil prices stay high, they leak into everything. Shipping, plastic, fertilizer, airline tickets. Paul's bus company pays more for fuel. Delivery trucks charge stores more. Stores charge Paul more. And the Fed faces a hard choice. Fight inflation with higher rates or hold back to protect jobs. Here's the question Paul really wants answered. Gas went up. Gas can come down. But why didn't the price of bread go back to 2020 levels? Because most prices are sticky downward. Some things do get cheaper over time. Televisions, computers, phone storage. That's because technology keeps making them easier to produce. But food, rent, and services rarely work that way. Once a bakery raises its price and customers keep paying, there's little reason to cut it. wages, rent, and contracts were all reset at the higher level. And central banks don't want prices to fall across the board. That's called deflation, and it can be worse than inflation. When prices fall, people wait to buy. Businesses earn less, they cut jobs, prices fall more. In the Great Depression, US prices fell by about a quarter in 4 years. It was a disaster. So, the realistic best case isn't lower prices, it's wages catching up. And there's a sneaky version of inflation, too. Shrinkflation. Same price, same package, a little less inside. So, who wins and who loses? The biggest loser is cash sitting still. There's a quick rule. Divide 72 by the inflation rate and you get the years it takes for prices to double. At 3% inflation, your cash loses half its buying power in about 24 years. Here's the hidden math on a savings account. If it pays 1% interest and inflation is 3%, you're actually losing about 2% a year. Economists call that your real return. It's the only return that matters. A dollar from early 2020 buys about 77 cents worth today. People on fixed incomes get squeezed, too. A pension that doesn't adjust buys less every single year. Social Security is different. It gets a cost of living raise every year to keep up with prices. In 2023, that raise was 8.7%, the biggest in four decades, but it only catches up after prices have already gone up. Retirees always feel at it first. Now, the winners, anyone with a fixed rate loan, your mortgage payment stays the same while your paycheck and the prices around it rise. Paul and Anne locked in their mortgage years ago. Inflation is quietly shrinking that debt. Owners of real things like homes and businesses often see their value rise with prices, which also means young people trying to buy their first home get hit twice. Higher prices and higher interest rates. And the biggest borrower of all, the government. Inflation shrinks the real value of national debt, which is one reason governments attempted to tolerate it. Workers are in the middle. When inflation jumps, wages usually lag behind. In 2021 and 2022, prices rose faster than most paychecks. That's why it felt so bad. Now, how bad can it get? In the 1970s, America lived through years of high inflation. It peaked at nearly 15% in 1980. Washington even tried freezing wages and prices by law in 1971. It didn't work. When the freeze ended, prices jumped again. To stop it, Fed Chair Paul Vulkar raised interest rates to around 20%. Mortgage rates hit 18%. Two recessions followed, but inflation finally broke. That's why today's Fed raises rates when inflation jumps. It's painful medicine, but they remember the 70s. And then there's hyperinflation. Germany, 1923. Prices were doubling every few days. People were paid twice a day and ran to spend it. Zimbabwe 2008. Prices spun so far out of control, the country ended up printing a $100 trillion note. Hyperinflation almost always has the same cause, a government printing money to pay its bills. That's why countries keep central banks independent from politicians. It's a guardrail. So, what does all this mean for someone in their 40s or 50s? First, think in real numbers. A 2% raise in a 3% year is a pay cut. When you ask for a raise, know the inflation number. It's your starting point, not a bonus. Second, cash is for emergencies, not for decades. A few months of expenses in cash is a safety net. 20 years of savings in cash is a slow leak. Money parked for 20 years needs to grow faster than prices or it shrinks. Third, some savings products are built for this. US Treasury Ibonds and TIPS are adjusted for inflation by design. They won't make you rich, but they're designed not to fall behind prices. Fourth, know your debts. Fixed rate debt gets lighter with inflation. Variable rate debt, like credit cards, gets heavier. In a high inflation period, paying off a credit card at 20% interest beats almost any savings account. And fifth, don't make big decisions based on one scary headline. Inflation moves in waves. What matters is the long run. This video is just general education. For your own situation, it's worth talking to a professional. That night, Paul sticks the old receipt on the fridge. Not because he's angry, because now he understands it. Inflation isn't a thief that broke into his kitchen. It's the slow, steady cost of an economy that keeps growing and sometimes overheating. The trick isn't to stop it. It's to make sure your money keeps up because prices will keep rising. The only question is whether you rise with them.
