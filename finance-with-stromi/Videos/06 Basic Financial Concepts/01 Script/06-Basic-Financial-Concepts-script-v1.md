- **NARRATOR:** Friday night. Nine fifteen. Theo is twenty-two, and he has just been paid for the first real job of his life. Two thousand dollars. He drove straight to his grandmother's house to show her.
- **Theo:** Nana. Two thousand dollars. I'm basically rich.

**[B02] nana6**: _Nana slides the bank app across the table: DEPOSIT $1,631.00. Theo's grin freezes. A moth bumps the lamp._

- **Nana:** You'll be paying his salary too.

**[B03] nana6**: _Nana pulls out a battered tin recipe box labelled THINGS NOBODY TELLS YOU. Ten index cards fan out across the table, one icon on each._

- **NARRATOR:** Nana June spent forty-one years behind a bank counter. She has watched people win and lose money in every way there is. And she says almost all of it comes down to ten ideas nobody teaches you. Taxes. Banks. Interest. Inflation. Recessions. Credit scores. Money itself. Investing. Value. And time.
- **Nana:** Sit down. We're doing all ten. Tonight.

## [B04] CARD: 10 Money Concepts / Nobody explains

## [B05] CARD: 1. Taxes

**[B06] nana6**: _Close on the stub. Each deduction line lifts off the paper as a little paper tag: SOCIAL SECURITY $124, MEDICARE $29, FEDERAL $156, STATE $60._

- **NARRATOR:** Let's find Theo's missing money. Social Security took six point two percent, a hundred and twenty-four dollars. Medicare, one point four five percent, twenty-nine dollars. Together that's called FICA, and almost every worker in America pays it from the very first dollar. Then federal income tax, about a hundred and fifty-six. And his state took about sixty.

**[B07] town6**: _Tabletop model town. Theo's paper tags fly in and become things: a road gets paved, a school bus starts, a fire truck rolls out, an old man on a bench gets a cheque._

- **NARRATOR:** Here's the simple version. Taxes are the membership fee for living in a country. Income tax is charged on the money you earn. Sales tax, on the money you spend. Capital gains tax, on the money your investments earn for you. That money builds roads, pays teachers and firefighters, and Social Security pays today's retirees. Medicare pays for their health care. When it's your turn, someone younger pays for yours.

**[B08] town6**: _Pip, a tiny road worker in the model town, pats a fresh pothole patch, then looks up at giant Theo._

- **Pip:** This pothole is sponsored by you, by the way.
- **Theo:** I paid a hundred and fifty-six dollars for that?
- **Pip:** No, you paid for about four inches of it.
- **Theo:** Which four inches?
- **Pip:** Don't step on them.

**[B09] nana6**: _Back in the kitchen. Nana taps a calendar: APRIL 15 circled in red. A little IRS envelope tag._

- **NARRATOR:** One more thing nobody warns you about. What comes out of your paycheck is only a guess, called withholding. Every spring you file a tax return, and you settle up. Paid too much, you get a refund. Paid too little, you owe the difference.
- **Theo:** So a refund is a present?
- **Nana:** A refund is your own money, coming back from a year-long loan you gave the government. At zero percent.

## [B10] CARD: 2. Banks

**[B11] bank**: _Old-fashioned bank lobby. Young Nana (flashback, same dress, darker hair) at the counter. Theo hands over a thousand-dollar bundle; it goes through a little door into the back._

- **NARRATOR:** So where does Theo's money go when he puts it in the bank? Most people picture a vault, with his thousand dollars sitting on a shelf with his name on it. That is not what happens. A bank is not a safe. It's a matchmaker.

**[B12] bank**: _Through the back door: the bundle is handed straight to Wendell, who is buying a jet ski. Theo watches from the counter._

- **NARRATOR:** The bank keeps a slice of your deposit ready for withdrawals, and lends most of the rest to someone else. It pays you a little interest for your money, charges the borrower a lot more for it, and keeps the difference. That gap is how a bank makes money.
- **Wendell:** Thanks for the jet ski, kid.
- **Theo:** That's my money!
- **Wendell:** Not right now it isn't.

**[B13] street**: _Night street outside a bank, long queue snaking round the block, umbrellas. Tag: SEPT 2008, WASHINGTON MUTUAL. Second tag fades in: MARCH 2023, $42 BILLION IN ONE DAY._

- **NARRATOR:** This works because not everyone wants their money back on the same day. When they do, it's called a bank run. In September two thousand eight, customers pulled about sixteen point seven billion dollars out of Washington Mutual in ten days, and it became the biggest bank failure in American history. In March twenty twenty-three, Silicon Valley Bank customers tried to pull out forty-two billion dollars in a single day, mostly from their phones. The bank was closed the next morning.

**[B14] vault**: _Big round vault door. A gold FDIC shield stamps onto the door: $250,000 PER DEPOSITOR, PER BANK._

- **NARRATOR:** So why trust a bank at all? Because in the US, deposits are insured by the FDIC, up to two hundred and fifty thousand dollars per depositor, per bank, for each type of account. Since it began in nineteen thirty-four, nobody has lost a single cent of insured money.
- **Theo:** So I'm safe.
- **Nana:** Up to two hundred and fifty thousand.
- **Theo:** Then I'm extremely safe.

## [B15] CARD: 3. Interest

**[B16] lender**: _Gus at a loan desk, rubber stamp ready. Theo holds a burrito and a credit card._

- **NARRATOR:** Interest is rent for money. When you borrow, you pay rent to use someone else's money. When you save, someone pays rent to use yours. Simple interest is charged only on the amount you started with. Compound interest is charged on that amount plus all the interest that has already piled up. Interest on interest.

**[B17] lender**: _Theo swipes for a $1,000 laptop. A small paper copy of the balance pops out every month, each one slightly bigger, stacking up behind him like a tower._

- **NARRATOR:** Credit card accounts that carry a balance are being charged about twenty-two percent a year on average, according to the Federal Reserve. Say Theo puts a thousand-dollar laptop on a card at that rate and only pays the twenty-five dollar minimum. It takes him about six years to clear it, and he pays roughly eighteen hundred and fifty dollars. For a laptop that died in year four.
- **Gus:** Minimum payment, sir?
- **Theo:** Sure, what's the catch?
- **Gus:** Nothing. See you in twenty thirty-two.

**[B18] tabletop**: _Kitchen table, a single coin. It splits into two, then four, then a little army of coins marching across the table._

- **NARRATOR:** Now flip it around. Money invested and growing at seven percent a year roughly doubles every ten years. A hundred dollars left alone for forty years at seven percent becomes about fifteen hundred. Same maths as the credit card. You just get to be the one collecting the rent.
- **Nana:** Interest is either your worst landlord, or your hardest-working employee.
- **Theo:** Can I be the landlord?
- **Nana:** That's the whole point of tonight.

## [B19] CARD: 4. Inflation

**[B20] store**: _Corner store. Dot at the till. Theo puts a bag of chips on the counter; it is visibly half air. Price tag flips from $3.99 to $4.79._

- **Theo:** This bag is half empty.
- **Dot:** It's half full of air. Premium air.
- **Theo:** And it costs more?
- **Dot:** Air's gone up.

**[B21] store**: _Shelf of products; each price tag ticks up. A five-dollar bill on the counter stays the same size but the shopping it buys shrinks._

- **NARRATOR:** That's inflation. Prices rising slowly across the whole economy, so the same dollar buys a little less every year. Your five-dollar bill still says five dollars. It just doesn't do as much. Since early twenty twenty, prices in America have gone up by around thirty percent in total. And in the latest full yearly reading, September, they were rising at about three point seven percent a year.

**[B22] stage**: _Auction stage. Mabel auctioneer, one TV on a plinth, a crowd waving cash. Price board jumps $500, $650, $800._

- **NARRATOR:** Why does it happen? Sometimes there's lots of money chasing the same few things. Picture one TV and a crowd of people who all just got paid.
- **Mabel:** Five hundred! Do I hear six fifty? Eight hundred, to the man in the very sad hat!
- **NARRATOR:** Sometimes it costs more to make things, because of a shortage or a broken supply chain. And sometimes people simply expect prices to rise, so they buy now, which pushes prices up. The Federal Reserve aims for about two percent a year. A little is normal. A lot quietly eats your savings.

**[B23] nana6**: _Kitchen. Nana opens a biscuit tin full of old cash. Theo leans in._

- **NARRATOR:** The Fed's main weapon is the interest rate. Raise it, and borrowing gets more expensive, so people and companies spend less, and prices cool down.
- **Theo:** So cash under the mattress is safe?
- **Nana:** Safe from burglars. Not from inflation. That tin has been losing a fight for thirty years.

## [B24] CARD: 5. Recessions

**[B25] club**: _A party in full swing. Disco ball, everyone dancing, Wendell buying rounds, Gus on the DJ deck. Tag: BOOM._

- **NARRATOR:** Think of the economy as a party. In the boom, everyone's hiring, everyone's spending, and the music keeps getting louder. Then, at some point, the lights flicker.

**[B26] club**: _Lights dim. Music slows to a sad tune. Wendell checks his phone, puts his wallet away. Dancers sit down. Tag: BUST._

- **Wendell:** Who ordered all these drinks?
- **Gus:** You did. Three hours ago. You were very confident.
- **NARRATOR:** Spending drops. Companies sell less, so they cut costs and lay people off. Those people spend even less. That loop is a recession.

**[B27] cork**: _Corkboard. A card reads: TWO QUARTERS OF FALLING GDP? It gets a red stamp: RULE OF THUMB. A second card: NBER DECIDES. Two dates pinned: 2008-09, 2020 (2 MONTHS)._

- **NARRATOR:** You may have heard a recession is two quarters in a row of the economy shrinking. That's a handy rule of thumb, but it isn't the official one. In the US, a panel at the National Bureau of Economic Research makes the call, by looking for a big drop in activity that spreads across the whole economy, and lasts more than a few months. The Great Recession ran from late two thousand seven to mid two thousand nine. The Covid recession in twenty twenty lasted just two months, the shortest on record.

**[B28] club**: _Morning. Mabel opens the blinds, Pip mops, the DJ deck starts again softly. Tag: RECOVERY._

- **NARRATOR:** And every recession so far has ended. Central banks cut interest rates, governments send out help, and slowly people start spending again. The party restarts. That's why Nana's first rule of recessions is boring: have a few months of bills saved before the lights flicker, not after.

## [B29] CARD: 6. Credit Scores

**[B30] desk3**: _Landlord's desk. Mabel the landlady with reading glasses studies a report card with Theo's face on it. A big dial: 300 to 850._

- **NARRATOR:** Your credit score is your money reputation, squeezed into one number. The common FICO score runs from three hundred to eight hundred and fifty. The national average is around seven hundred and fourteen. It isn't a measure of how rich you are. It's a guess at how likely you are to pay back what you borrow, and landlords, lenders, and sometimes even phone companies look at it.
- **Mabel:** No history at all?
- **Theo:** I've never borrowed a dollar in my life.
- **Mabel:** Hmm. Suspicious.

**[B31] desk3**: _The report card opens into a pie with five slices that pop up one by one: PAYMENT HISTORY 35%, AMOUNTS OWED 30%, LENGTH 15%, NEW CREDIT 10%, MIX 10%._

- **NARRATOR:** Here is what goes into it. Payment history, about thirty-five percent. Do you pay on time? How much you owe compared with your limits, about thirty percent. How long you've had credit, about fifteen. New applications, ten. And the mix of credit types, ten. That means the single biggest move is the dull one: never pay late. Set up autopay for at least the minimum, and keep your card balances well below the limit.

**[B32] nana6**: _Kitchen. Nana fishes a credit card out of her purse, older than Theo, with a 1986 date on it._

- **Theo:** Why do you still have that ancient card?
- **Nana:** Because it's forty years old, and the score loves old things.
- **Theo:** Like you.
- **Nana:** Careful. I'm still in the will.

## [B33] CARD: 7. What Money Is

**[B34] island6**: _Tropical island at golden hour. Giant stone discs with holes lean against palm trees. Pip, as an islander, rolls one along the path._

- **NARRATOR:** Here's a strange idea. Money only works because we all agree it does. On the Pacific island of Yap, people used giant stone discs called rai as money. Some were taller than a person. They were so heavy that when one changed hands, it often stayed exactly where it was. Everyone just remembered who owned it now.

**[B35] island6**: _Dot points out to sea, where a stone disc sits underwater, a fish swimming through the hole._

- **NARRATOR:** There's even a story of a huge stone that sank to the bottom of the sea while it was being brought in by canoe. Nobody ever saw it again. The island kept counting it as money anyway.
- **Dot:** So the richest family on the island owns a rock nobody can see?
- **Pip:** Yep.
- **Dot:** That's ridiculous.
- **Pip:** What's in your bank account?
- **Dot:** ...Numbers on a screen.
- **Pip:** Mm-hm.

**[B36] marble**: _Marble hall, a dollar bill on a pedestal beside a gold bar under glass. The gold bar slides away. Tag: AUG 15, 1971._

- **NARRATOR:** The dollar used to be tied to gold. In August nineteen seventy-one, President Nixon cut that link. Since then the dollar has been backed by one thing: trust. Trust that the government will keep it stable, and that the Federal Reserve won't let too much or too little of it slosh around. Print too much and prices run away. Keep too little and the economy chokes. Money is an agreement. And like any agreement, it only lasts as long as people keep believing in it.

## [B37] CARD: 8. Investing

**[B38] orchard6**: _A sunny orchard. Theo holds a bag of seed money. Nana in a gardening hat with a watering can._

- **NARRATOR:** If inflation slowly shrinks money that sits still, investing is how you make money move. Instead of trading your hours for money, you trade money for a share of something that grows. Here are the four big buckets.

**[B39] orchard6**: _Four little signposts pop up, each with a prop: a slice of pie (STOCKS), an IOU note (BONDS), a fruit basket (FUNDS), a tiny house (REAL ESTATE)._

- **NARRATOR:** Stocks: a tiny slice of a company. If the company grows, your slice is worth more. Bonds: you lend money to a government or a company, and they pay you back with interest. Funds: one basket holding hundreds of stocks or bonds at once, so one bad apple can't sink you. And real estate: property that earns rent or rises in value.

**[B40] orchard6**: _Theo plants a seed and stares at it. Nothing happens. He taps his foot. Still nothing. Pip walks past with a wheelbarrow._

- **Theo:** It's not doing anything.
- **Pip:** It's a tree, not a microwave.
- **Theo:** How long?
- **Pip:** Come back when you're forty.

**[B41] trading**: _Trading floor: a long price line climbs with sharp dips, each dip labelled 2000, 2008, 2020. Little Theo rides it like a roller coaster._

- **NARRATOR:** Investing has risk. Markets fall, sometimes hard, and nobody can tell you when. But over long stretches, a broad basket of US companies has grown at roughly seven percent a year after inflation, on average, dips included. The bigger danger for most people isn't a bad year. It's never starting, and letting inflation nibble away at money that never moved.

## [B42] CARD: 9. Value

**[B43] desert6**: _Blazing desert, heat shimmer. Wendell crawls in, dusty, clutching a velvet bag of diamonds. Dot runs a lemonade-style stand selling WATER._

- **NARRATOR:** Water keeps you alive. Diamonds don't. So why is a diamond worth thousands and a glass of water almost nothing? People have been arguing about that for more than two hundred and fifty years. The answer is that price isn't about how useful something is in general. It's about how badly someone wants the next one, and how hard it is to get.
- **Wendell:** Water. Please. I'll give you a diamond.
- **Dot:** Two diamonds.
- **Wendell:** You're a monster.
- **Dot:** I'm a market.

**[B44] desert6**: _Dot's stand sign changes: WATER 2 DIAMONDS. A rain cloud drifts over. Price drops to 1 PEBBLE._

- **NARRATOR:** This is the most useful idea in the whole list. You get paid in proportion to the value you provide and how hard you are to replace. A surgeon earns more than a cashier not because they work harder, but because fewer people can do what they do, and the stakes are higher. A brand can charge ten times more for the same bag, because people believe it's worth more. If you want to earn more, the question is never just how many hours. It's what problem can I solve that few other people can.

## [B45] CARD: 10. Time

**[B46] clock6**: _Inside a giant clock tower, gears turning. Two savers on a beam: Pip at 25 with a small jar, Gus at 35 with a bigger jar. Age tags over each._

- **NARRATOR:** Which brings us to the last idea, and the most powerful one. Time. Meet two savers. Pip starts at twenty-five. He puts two hundred dollars a month into an investment growing at seven percent a year. At thirty-five, he stops. Never adds another cent. Gus starts at thirty-five, the year Pip stops, puts in the same two hundred a month, and keeps going for thirty years, until sixty-five.

**[B47] clock6**: _Gears spin fast; both jars swell. At 65: Pip's jar tag $281,000 (put in $24,000), Gus's $244,000 (put in $72,000)._

- **NARRATOR:** Pip put in twenty-four thousand dollars. Gus put in seventy-two thousand, three times as much. At sixty-five, Pip has about two hundred and eighty-one thousand. Gus has about two hundred and forty-four thousand. The man who saved for ten years beats the man who saved for thirty, because his money had ten extra years to compound.
- **Gus:** I put in three times as much!
- **Pip:** I put in ten more birthdays.

**[B48] clock6**: _Slow pull back from the gears. Theo stands small at the bottom, watching the hands sweep._

- **NARRATOR:** Most people trade time for money, an hour for a paycheck. Investing flips that. Every year you wait costs you more than the year before. And at twenty-two, Theo has something nobody can buy back. He has the most time he will ever have.

## [B49] CARD: Your Wallet

**[B50] nana6**: _Back in Nana's kitchen, near midnight. Ten index cards laid out. Theo has written a plan on the back of the paystub._

- **NARRATOR:** So here are the ten ideas, as one plan for your own wallet. Know where your paycheck goes before it arrives. Keep your cash in an insured bank, and a few months of bills saved for the day the party stops. Kill any debt charging you twenty percent. Pay every bill on time, so your score works for you. And put something, even small, into a low-cost, broad fund, as early as you can, then leave it alone. Inflation is working against money that sits still. Time works for money that moves.

**[B51] nana6**: _Theo folds the paystub, sets up autopay on his phone, moves $100 to a fund. Nana pours two cups of tea._

- **Theo:** Okay. A hundred dollars a month into a fund. Autopay on. And I'm not touching it.
- **Nana:** Good boy. Now. The three hundred and sixty-nine dollars.
- **Theo:** I know, I know. Roads, schools, pothole number four.
- **Nana:** And one more thing.

**[B52] nana6**: _Nana slides a grocery receipt across the table. Theo sighs and reaches for his wallet. Lamp flickers, camera pulls back out of the window into the night._

- **Nana:** You ate my lasagne. That's twelve dollars.
- **Theo:** Nana!
- **Nana:** Inflation, sweetheart. It was nine last year.
- **NARRATOR:** Two thousand dollars. Sixteen hundred and thirty-one in the bank. And for the first time, Theo knows exactly where every dollar went, and where the next ones are going.
