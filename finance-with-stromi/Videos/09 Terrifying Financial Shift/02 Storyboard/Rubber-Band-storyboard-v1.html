The Rubber Band: the yen carry trade

An original Stromi script for Finance video 9 ("Most Terrifying Financial Shift in the World Right Now"). The transcript you sent was research only. Story, skits and every line are new, built around one image: a giant rubber band stretched across the Pacific from a tiny 0% lending window in Tokyo to a New York trading desk. It snapped on 5 Aug 2024 and has been stretched again. Skit lines (in red) sit between narration like Two Thursdays and Frozen Market. Circular ending: "Nothing happens in summer" and Kenji reaching for the dial again. The thriller tension stays, but it is framed as a risk, not a prophecy, and the source's alarmist claims ("rigged", "cover-up", "your 401(k) gets decimated") are dropped. Title ideas: "The Rubber Band That Crashed Japan in a Day (It's Stretched Again)", "Japan Just Started Selling America's Debt", "Most Terrifying Financial Shift in the World Right Now".

Narrator To understand the band, start with Japan. For most of thirty years, Japan's prices barely moved, so its central bank kept interest rates at almost nothing. From twenty sixteen it went further and pushed them below zero. Borrowing in yen was the cheapest money on Earth.

Y08New York trading floor1:37

New York trading floor, all glass and screens. Chet slides in on an office chair, a suitcase of yen in one hand. He swaps it at a currency booth for a suitcase of dollars and drops them into US bonds and big tech stocks.

Narrator Now meet Chet. Chet's job is to notice free money. So he borrows yen at almost zero, swaps it into dollars, and buys American things that pay more. US government bonds paying four or five percent. Big tech stocks that kept going up. That's it. That's the whole trick. Borrow cheap in one country, invest in another, keep the difference. It's called the carry trade.

Chet I borrow at zero, I earn five. I'm not a genius. I'm just awake.

Y09Pacific globe-table + rubber band2:07

Back on the globe-table. Every time Chet borrows, the rubber band stretches a little further across the Pacific. Dot measures it with a tape measure that runs out.

Narrator And it isn't just Chet. Hedge funds, banks, traders all over the world did the same thing, for years. How big did it get? Nobody knows. Estimates run from a few hundred billion dollars to well over a trillion. The band was stretched by people who don't publish their positions.

Dot How long is it now?

Gus Tokyo to New York.

Dot And how long was it meant to be?

Gus About this.

Y10New York trading floor2:34

Close on Chet's screen: a USD/JPY line drifting sideways, a little green PROFIT counter ticking up. A tiny warning light labelled YEN blinks in the corner, ignored.

Narrator There's one catch, and it's the whole story. Chet borrowed yen, so one day he has to pay back yen. If the yen suddenly gets stronger, every yen he owes costs him more dollars. As long as the yen stays weak, the trick works. The day the yen jumps, the trick bites.

Pip What's that blinking light?

Chet Decoration.

2. The Multiplier

Chapter card, 3 s, piano sting + whoosh.

Y12Broker back office2:59

A broker's back office with velvet ropes. Chet hands over one gold coin labelled $1,000. A clerk stacks ten coins in front of him: $10,000. A sign on the wall: 10 TO 1.

Narrator Some players didn't stop at borrowing yen. They borrowed again, on top. It's called leverage. You put down a small deposit, the broker lends you the rest. At ten to one, one thousand dollars of your money controls a ten thousand dollar position.

Chet One coin goes in, ten coins come out. I love this room.

Y13Broker back office3:21

A big chalkboard. +5% arrow: the stack grows by $500, and Chet's single coin glows +50%. Then a -5% arrow: the stack loses $500, and half of Chet's coin crumbles away. Then -10%: the coin is gone.

Narrator Here's the math. If what you bought rises five percent, ten thousand becomes ten thousand five hundred. You made five hundred dollars on a thousand. That's fifty percent. Without leverage you'd have made fifty bucks.

Narrator But it works the same way down. A five percent drop wipes out half your money. A ten percent drop and your deposit is gone. All of it. And at that moment someone else decides what happens next.

Y14Broker back office3:49

The door opens. Brick, an enormous collector in a tiny bowler hat, ducks in. He speaks softly and holds a phone the size of a brick that is ringing.

Brick Hello, Chet. This is your margin call.

Chet Can I call you back?

Brick I am the call back.

Chet What if I wait for prices to recover?

Brick You can wait. Your stocks can't. I'm selling them now. Whatever price I get.

Y15Broker back office4:05

Brick carries Chet's stack of stocks out to the street and sells them off a folding table at half price. Passers-by grab bargains. Prices on a board behind him fall further each time.

Narrator That's a margin call. When losses eat your deposit, the broker sells your stuff for you, fast, at whatever price buyers will pay. Now picture thousands of Bricks, all selling at once, into the same few buyers. Selling pushes prices down. Lower prices trigger more margin calls. More calls, more selling. The higher the leverage, the faster the loop spins.
