## 2. What Money Really Is

**S14** [market] VISUAL: An old-fashioned market stall. Wendell with a mountain of apples. Dot arrives holding scissors and a comb.
- NARRATOR: The textbook answer: money is a medium of exchange. A tool that lets us trade without carrying chickens around. Before it, you had to find someone who had what you wanted and wanted what you had.
- WENDELL: Apples. Ten for a haircut.
- DOT: I don't want apples.
- WENDELL: What do you want?
- DOT: Shoes.
- WENDELL: I don't have shoes.
- DOT: Then you're getting a very bad haircut.

**S15** [market] VISUAL: Wendell hands Dot a coin instead; the coin glows and turns into a little tag: VALUE. Dot walks off happy toward a shoe stall.
- NARRATOR: Money solves that. But medium of exchange doesn't tell you what money actually is. Here's a more useful way to see it. Money is a receipt for value. When you hand over twenty dollars for a book, you're saying, this is worth twenty dollars of my life to me. When your boss pays you, they're saying your hours were worth that much to them.

**S16** [cork] VISUAL: Corkboard of sticky notes. One note gets circled: MONEY = VALUE. A second note pinned below: "money is the root of all evil" with a red pen correction adding "THE LOVE OF".
- NARRATOR: Why does that matter? Because a lot of us carry around a quiet belief that money is a bit dirty. You've heard the line, money is the root of all evil. Except that's not even the full quote. The line comes from the Bible, and it says the love of money is the root of all kinds of evil. Two words, big difference.

**S17** [diner] VISUAL: A diner booth. Gus and Dot look at a rich man's photo in a magazine.
- GUS: Look at this guy. Bet he cheated somebody.
- DOT: He invented a better fridge.
- GUS: Bet he cheated the old fridge.
- NARRATOR: When we see someone wealthy, we tend to ask who lost so they could win. We rarely ask what they made that people were willing to pay for.

**S18** [diner] VISUAL: Two-way split: a scammer with a shiny fake watch on one side, a baker with bread on the other. Both holding money.
- NARRATOR: To be clear, there are crooks. Scammers convince people that something worthless is valuable. But that tells you about the scammer, not about money. Money is a tool, like a hammer. You can build a house with it or break a window. Mostly, it buys you options. What you do with those options is the part that's about you.

**S19** [apartment] VISUAL: Back with Jimmy, phone still at $11.40. He looks at the camera.

## 3. Money In, Money Out

**S21** [tabletop] VISUAL: A big bathtub prop on a tabletop: a gold tap labelled WORK pours in; a drain labelled SPENDING pulls water out. Water level = NET WORTH.
- NARRATOR: Picture a bathtub. The tap is everything you produce. For most of us, that's our job: we trade time and skill for a paycheck. The drain is everything you consume. Rent, groceries, streaming, the car, the takeout. And the water left in the tub? That's your net worth. Everything you own, minus everything you owe.

**S22** [tabletop] VISUAL: Jimmy's tub: a fire-hose tap pouring hard, a giant drain swirling, the tub nearly empty. Carla's tub next to it: a small steady tap, a tiny drain, tub half full.
- NARRATOR: Jimmy has a fire hose for a tap. But he also has a drain the size of a manhole. Carla's tap is a garden hose. Her drain is a straw. Guess whose tub is fuller.
- JIMMY: Okay, but my tap is really impressive.
- CARLA: Your floor is wet, Jimmy.

**S23** [kitchen3] VISUAL: Carla at her kitchen table with her notebook. Narrator asks the viewer directly; camera pushes toward the notebook.
- NARRATOR: So try this. Think about all the money that's come into your life. Every paycheck since your first job. Now, how much of it is still with you today? In savings, in something you own, in an investment? For most people, the honest answer is, not much. And it's usually not because the tap was too small.

**S24** [office3] VISUAL: The old wall survey from 2017 shown as a yellowed poster: 78% PAYCHECK TO PAYCHECK; 1 in 10 of $100K+ earners. Pinned next to the new 2026 jar chart.
- NARRATOR: This isn't new, either. Back in twenty seventeen, a CareerBuilder survey found that nearly eight in ten American workers lived paycheck to paycheck, and about one in ten people earning over a hundred thousand did too. Almost a decade later, the numbers barely moved. If you earn far more than the cost of living, and still spend all of it, the problem isn't the tap.

## 4. The Leak

**S26** [carlot] VISUAL: Car lot. Jimmy, just promoted, walks in grinning; Wendell the dealer spreads his arms in front of a shiny red sports car.
- NARRATOR: Here's how the drain grows, and it happens to almost everyone. Last spring, Jimmy got a raise. Ten thousand dollars a year.
- WENDELL: Congratulations on the promotion. You deserve this.
- JIMMY: I deserve this.
- WENDELL: Seventy-two months. Low monthly payment.
- JIMMY: How much is it all together?
- WENDELL: Low monthly payment.

**S27** [apartment] VISUAL: Time-lapse in Jimmy's apartment: a bigger TV slides in, a gym membership card, a fancier coffee machine, more subscriptions popping up as little tags on the wall. The tub level stays flat.
- NARRATOR: Economists call this lifestyle creep. Every raise gets matched by a new monthly bill. A bigger place. A nicer car. One more subscription. Each one feels small and deserved. Add them up, and the raise disappears before it ever reaches the tub. The more you earn, the more you need to earn, just to stand still.

**S28** [ring] VISUAL: Back in the dark arena. A photo-card exhibit on easels (no animated likeness): mansion, tiger silhouette, row of cars, a giant pile of bills.

In his bankruptcy, he owed the I R S alone more than thirteen million dollars. But there were also the mansions, the cars, and, famously, pet tigers.

## 5. The Real Rat Race

**S32** [hall] VISUAL: A marble hall with a cheap stage. Gus as a flashy guru in a shiny suit, a banner: ESCAPE THE RAT RACE MASTERCLASS, $1,997. A hamster wheel spins behind him.
- NARRATOR: Now, the phrase in the title. The rat race. An endless, exhausting race that goes nowhere. You've probably heard it from somebody selling something.
- GUS: Still working a nine-to-five? You're a rat on a wheel!
- DOT: I like my job.
- GUS: That's what the wheel wants you to think. Course is nineteen ninety-seven.
- DOT: Is that monthly?
- GUS: ...It can be.

**S33** [hall] VISUAL: The guru's banner tears down to reveal a plain sign behind it: THE JOB IS NOT THE TRAP.
- NARRATOR: Here's my problem with that pitch. A steady job isn't the rat race. Plenty of people love their work, or are happy with it, or use it to fund something they care about. Calling every employee a rat is just a way to sell a course to the ones who feel stuck.

**S34** [apartment] VISUAL: Jimmy on a hamster wheel in his apartment, running in a suit, the wheel attached to a paycheck dispenser that drops one bill per lap, straight into a bills tray.
- NARRATOR: The real rat race is living on the edge. Being one paycheck away from zero. Money arrives, and it's gone before you can feel it. And the scary part is what happens when something goes wrong. A layoff. A hospital visit. A car that won't start.

**S35** [garage3] VISUAL: A garage. Jimmy's red sports car with the hood up, smoke. Ray the mechanic hands him a long invoice: $1,400.

**S36** [office3] VISUAL: Wall chart: 63% COULD COVER $400 WITH CASH. The other 37% drawn as people holding credit cards, or empty pockets. Tag: FEDERAL RESERVE, MAY 2026.
- NARRATOR: The Federal Reserve asks Americans every year: if you got a surprise four-hundred-dollar bill, could you cover it with cash or its equivalent? In the latest report, sixty-three percent said yes. That means more than a third of adults can't handle a four-hundred-dollar surprise without borrowing, selling something, or not paying at all.

**S37** [apartment] VISUAL: Jimmy alone at night, phone glowing, ceiling fan turning. Quote card pinned to his fridge: "The things you own end up owning you." Tyler Durden, Fight Club.
- NARRATOR: And living on that edge changes how you feel about everything. Your job stops being a choice and becomes a lifeline. You can't quit a bad boss. You can't take a risk on a better idea. As a certain movie character once put it, the things you own end up owning you.

**S38** [kitchen3] VISUAL: Carla calmly pays a car repair bill from her jar; she tapes the receipt into her notebook.

- CARLA: Fourteen hundred? Okay. The jar takes it.

## 6. The Ostrich

**S40** [living3] VISUAL: Morning after a night out. Jimmy on the couch, phone face down on the coffee table. He reaches for it, pulls his hand back.
- NARRATOR: So how does anybody get off the wheel? The first step sounds embarrassingly simple. Look. Write down where your money actually goes. Every month. Housing, transport, food, bills, fun, debt. But there's a reason most people never do it.

**S41** [living3] VISUAL: Jimmy slowly lowers his head under a couch cushion. Dot enters with coffee and stares.
- DOT: What are you doing?
- JIMMY: Checking my bank app.
- DOT: Your head is in the couch.
- JIMMY: If I don't look, the number can't hurt me.

**S42** [study] VISUAL: A researcher's desk with a little ostrich figurine burying its head in a sand tray. Tag: THE OSTRICH EFFECT.
- NARRATOR: Behavioural economists have a name for this. The ostrich effect. One well-known study found that investors log in to look at their accounts more when the market is up, and less when it's down. We love checking good news. We avoid bad news, even when the bad news is exactly what we need to see.

**S43** [kitchen3] VISUAL: Carla's notebook opened in close-up: neat columns, categories, a red circle around TAKEOUT $410.
- NARRATOR: Carla isn't braver than Jimmy. She just built a habit before she needed it. Twenty minutes, once a month, with a notebook. And the first time she did it, years ago, she found something nobody had told her about.
- CARLA: Four hundred and ten dollars. On takeout. In one month.
- DOT: That's a lot of noodles.
- CARLA: That's a lot of noodles I don't remember.

**S44** [living3] VISUAL: Jimmy lifts his head out of the cushion and flips the phone over. Slow push-in on his face as the screen lights him.
- NARRATOR: Looking is the hardest part, and it's also the cheapest. You can't fix a leak you refuse to look at.

## 7. The Plan School Skipped

**S46** [classroom] VISUAL: A school classroom. Chalkboard: algebra, the periodic table, the dates of old wars. Mabel the teacher. Jimmy as a grown man squeezed into a tiny desk raises his hand.
- JIMMY: Will this be on the test?
- MABEL: Which part?
- JIMMY: The part about credit cards.
- MABEL: We don't do that part.
- NARRATOR: For most adults today, money simply wasn't on the syllabus. That's changing. By this year, about thirty US states have passed laws requiring a personal finance course to graduate high school. But if you're past high school, nobody's coming back to teach you. So here's the course, in five steps.

**S47** [classroom] VISUAL: Chalkboard wipes clean. Mabel writes 1. TRACK IT. 2. GIVE EVERY DOLLAR A JOB.
- NARRATOR: One. Track it. That's the notebook we just talked about, or an app, or a spreadsheet. Whatever you'll actually open.
- NARRATOR: Two. Give every dollar a job before the month starts. That's all a budget is. Rent gets this much, food gets this much, fun gets this much. And the one rule that matters: the plan has to spend less than you earn. Living below your means isn't a punishment. It's the gap where your future lives.

**S48** [kitchen3] VISUAL: Carla fills a jar labelled EMERGENCY; a progress ring around it fills from one month to six.
- NARRATOR: Three. Build a cushion. Start with a small emergency fund, even a thousand dollars, then grow it toward three to six months of essential bills. That's what turns a car repair from a disaster into a Tuesday.

**S49** [bank] VISUAL: Banker's office. A credit card with a little meter showing 22% APR glowing red. Jimmy cuts a paper chain labelled CARD BALANCE link by link.
- NARRATOR: Four. Kill expensive debt. The average interest rate on credit cards that charge interest is above twenty-two percent right now. No ordinary investment reliably pays you twenty-two percent a year. So paying off that card is one of the best returns you'll ever get.
- MABEL: You're paying more than the minimum?
- JIMMY: I'm paying more than the minimum.
- MABEL: I've never seen this before. Can I take a picture?

**S50** [tabletop] VISUAL: The bathtub again. The drain gets a plug; the water rises; a second little pipe from the tub feeds a small tree labelled INVESTED that starts to grow.
- NARRATOR: And five. Now, finally, work on the tap, and put the gap to work. Grow your skills, ask for the raise, take the better job. But when the raise comes, send at least half of it straight to savings or investments before it ever lands in checking. That way the drain never gets the chance to grow. That's the whole framework. Fix the drain first. Then open the tap.

## Your Wallet (Four questions)

**S52** [wallet] VISUAL: Close on an open wallet on a table. Four tags pop out of it one at a time, matching each question.

- NARRATOR: If any of those made you want to put your head in the couch, that's the one to start with.
