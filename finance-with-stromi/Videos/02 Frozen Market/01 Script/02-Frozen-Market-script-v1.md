## CHAPTER 2: THE RULE THAT BROKE (1:55-3:25)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: Start with the one rule everyone remembers from school. Supply and demand. More sellers than buyers, sellers compete, prices drop until somebody bites.
SKIT (a market stall. WENDELL sells apples, a mountain of them. One customer, DOT, wanders by.)
WENDELL: Apples! A dollar! ...Ninety cents! ...Eighty! For you, seventy!
DOT (picks one up, puts it back): I'll think about it.
WENDELL (to camera, sweating): That's how it's supposed to work.

NARRATOR: And in a lot of places, it is working. In September, Redfin counted forty-one of the fifty biggest metro areas as buyer's markets. A record. In Miami, sellers outnumber buyers more than two and a half to one. Tampa, about two to one.
VISUAL: Quote card, typewriter text on a cream card pinned to a corkboard: "A buyer's market born out of caution, not strength." Asad Khan, Senior Economist, Redfin.
NARRATOR: Redfin's senior economist Asad Khan called it "a buyer's market born out of caution, not strength." Translation: there aren't suddenly loads of homes. Buyers just quietly walked out of the shop.
NARRATOR: Why? Mortgage rates. In late September they jumped back above seven percent for the first time since early 2025. A war with Iran sent oil up, inflation fears sent government bond yields up, and mortgages ride on those yields. Today they're sitting around seven point two. **[CHECK AT RECORD: latest Freddie Mac weekly rate]**
NARRATOR: And yet the typical existing home still sold for four hundred seventeen thousand five hundred dollars in September. Higher than a year before, for the thirty-ninth month in a row. **[CHECK AT RECORD: NAR October report]**
NARRATOR: So why isn't the rule working? Because it assumes sellers actually want to sell. And the strangest thing about this market is the millions of people who simply don't.

## CHAPTER 3: GOLDEN HANDCUFFS (3:25-5:20)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: Back to Linda. In 2021, mortgage rates hit the lowest levels in American history. Millions locked in around three percent. And in America, most mortgages are fixed for thirty years. The rate you sign on day one is the rate you keep.
VISUAL: Linda's kitchen table, warm lamp. A calculator, two receipts side by side.
NARRATOR: Let's run her numbers. Four hundred thousand dollars borrowed at three percent: about sixteen hundred ninety a month. Borrow the same amount today at about seven point two: about two thousand seven hundred.

LINDA (tapping the calculator): It's a thousand dollars a month more.
GUS: For a smaller house?
LINDA: For a smaller house. (She puts the flyer under the fruit bowl.)

NARRATOR: More than twelve thousand dollars a year, just for the privilege of moving. That's why people call these golden handcuffs. Linda's cheap loan is the best deal she'll ever get. It's also the thing keeping her in a house that doesn't fit her life anymore.

NARRATOR: And she's got company. Roughly four in ten American homeowners don't have a mortgage at all. No payment, no pressure. ## CHAPTER 4: THE BAKER WHO CAN'T WAIT (5:20-7:05)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: But one kind of seller can't wait at all. Home builders. To a family, a house is a home. To a builder, it's inventory. Like bread.
SKIT (a bakery at closing time. GUS the baker in a hard hat stares at shelves of house-shaped loaves. The clock ticks toward 9.)
GUS: Fresh this morning.
DOT (customer): And tomorrow?
GUS: Tomorrow it's yesterday's bread. (He slaps a "SPECIAL OFFER" sticker on a loaf.)

NARRATOR: Builders borrow to buy land and build, and their loans are nothing like a cheap thirty-year mortgage. Every month a finished house sits empty costs them money. And the number of finished, unsold new homes just reached its highest level since 2009.
NARRATOR: So builders got creative. Some cut prices. But many do something sneakier. Instead of cutting the price of the house, they cut the price of the loan. It's called a rate buydown. The builder pays the lender up front, and the buyer gets a mortgage at, say, five percent instead of seven.
VISUAL: A price tag on a house reads $450,000 and never moves. Behind it, a hand slides a fat envelope across a lender's desk.
NARRATOR: On paper, the house sold at full price. In reality, the builder might have spent tens of thousands making it happen. That money never shows up in the headline. A home that "sold for four fifty" may really have sold for something like four twenty. The price tag didn't move. The deal did.
NARRATOR: And builders are discounting so hard that the typical brand-new home now sells for less than the typical existing one. That's like a brand-new car costing less than a used one with sixty thousand miles on it.
SKIT (a car lot. WENDELL the dealer points at a shiny new car and a dusty old one.)
WENDELL: This one, new. Cheaper. That one, sixty thousand miles. More expensive.
PIP: ...Why?
WENDELL (leaning in): Nobody wants to give up the old one's financing.

NARRATOR: Which raises an uncomfortable question. If the sellers who can't wait are hiding their discounts, what are prices really doing?

The average looks strong while millions of people can't afford anything at all.
NARRATOR: Second trap: America isn't one housing market. It's hundreds of little ones. In the pandemic, millions moved to the Sun Belt. Builders followed and built like crazy. Then the people stopped coming.

NARRATOR: Third, and honestly my favourite: the prices in the headlines are sold prices. Deals that were often agreed two or three months ago. That's the rear-view mirror. If you want the windshield, look at asking prices, what sellers are writing on the sign today.

NARRATOR: Now the part almost nobody talks about. Inflation. S&P's Case-Shiller index says national home prices rose two point one percent over the year to August. Sounds fine. ## CHAPTER 6: UNDERWATER (9:50-12:20)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: Danielle bought in Austin in 2023, near the top, with a small down payment. Now her home is worth less than she owes. That's called being underwater.
VISUAL: Danielle's living room slowly fills with blue water, rising past the couch. She sits calmly in nurse scrubs, holding a mug above the waterline.
NARRATOR: And here's the trap. If you're underwater, selling doesn't get you out. If Danielle sold today, the sale wouldn't cover her loan. She'd have to bring cash to the closing table just to leave. So she's stuck too, for the opposite reason to Linda.
SKIT (estate agent's office. WENDELL in a suit hands Danielle a sheet.)
WENDELL: Good news! We found a buyer!
DANIELLE (reading): And I... owe you nineteen thousand dollars?
WENDELL: That's the good news part. (He offers her a mint.)

NARRATOR: How many Danielles are there? According to mortgage data firm ICE, about eight hundred thirteen thousand borrowers were underwater by the middle of this year, up forty-four percent in a single year. Eighty-five percent of them took out their loans in 2022 or later. Texas and Florida alone are thirty-nine percent of them. And three out of four used low-down-payment government-backed loans, FHA or VA.
NARRATOR: Some are starting to slip. In June, five point seven percent of FHA borrowers were at least ninety days late or in foreclosure, almost two points higher than a year before. Foreclosures nationwide hit a six-year high, though still below where they were before the pandemic.

NARRATOR: And then, at the end of October, something made me raise an eyebrow. Zillow's mortgage arm said it would cover the entire down payment for some FHA buyers. Up to three point five percent of the price, which is the full FHA minimum. It comes as a second loan, zero percent interest, no monthly payment. Live in the home five years and make every payment on time, and it's forgiven.
VISUAL: A gift box with a bow labelled "3.5% DOWN". A long string from the bow trails off-screen, tied to a tiny anvil.
NARRATOR: But there are strings. Sell, move out or refinance before year five, and the whole thing comes due. You need a credit score of at least six forty. And it isn't offered in seven states, including Florida, New York and Arizona.
NARRATOR: Let me argue both sides. For: the biggest wall for first-time buyers usually isn't the monthly payment, it's the lump sum. Plenty of renters already pay as much as a mortgage would cost. They just can't save while paying rent. This knocks that wall down, and buyers still have to qualify for the full payment.
NARRATOR: Against: think about Danielle. She put very little down, and when Austin dipped, she was underwater almost immediately. A buyer who puts none of their own money in starts with no cushion at all. And three out of four underwater borrowers already have FHA or VA loans. It's not 2008-style "no income check" lending. But it is one more way of fixing an affordability problem by adding debt.

## CHAPTER 7: IS THIS 2008? (12:20-13:35)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: So, is this 2008 all over again? Mostly, no. And it's worth being clear, because a lot of people online are selling fear right now.
VISUAL: Split screen. Left, sepia 2006: a bank teller tosses loans out of a window like confetti. Right, today: a teller with a magnifying glass checks a pay slip.
NARRATOR: In 2008, banks lent to people who couldn't afford it. Some loans didn't even check your income. Many started with a tiny teaser payment that exploded after two years. Wall Street bundled them, sold them worldwide, and when people stopped paying, the whole system cracked.
NARRATOR: Today is different in three big ways. One: after 2008, lending rules got much stricter. Lenders check income, jobs and savings. Two: most mortgages are fixed. There's no wave of exploding payments waiting. Three: owners are sitting on a huge cushion. According to ICE, Americans with mortgages hold more than eighteen trillion dollars in home equity, a record. Even if prices fell meaningfully, most would still be above water.
NARRATOR: So no, the banking system doesn't look like it's about to collapse. But there is a crack. It's small, and it runs right through the youngest, newest owners. The Danielles. The people who did exactly what everyone told them to do, at exactly the wrong time.
NARRATOR: And the thing that might be worse than 2008 isn't a crack at all.

## CHAPTER 8: MUSICAL CHAIRS (13:35-15:20)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: 2008 was an explosion. Loud, fast, impossible to miss. Prices crashed, people got hurt, and then, painfully, the market reset. Homes got cheaper, and a new generation could eventually buy. What's happening now isn't an explosion. It's a freeze.
SKIT (a church hall, a game of musical chairs. Music plays. LINDA and a row of older players sit firmly in chairs, knitting. MARCUS circles alone.)
MARCUS (circling): Isn't everyone supposed to get up?
LINDA (not looking up from her knitting): The music's still playing, dear.
(Music stops. Marcus is the only one standing. The music starts again. He keeps walking.)

NARRATOR: That's Marcus. NAR's brand-new survey, out this month, says the typical first-time buyer in America is now forty years old, tying the oldest ever recorded. First-time buyers are just twenty-two percent of purchases, barely up from last year's record low, and roughly half the share of 2007. The typical repeat buyer? Sixty-two. So most homes are being bought by people who already own one: they sell, take the equity, buy the next, often in cash.
NARRATOR: And the young people who do get in? More and more, they have help. A record one in ten first-time buyers paid all cash, and inheritance just hit a record share of down payments. The bank of Mom and Dad, and sometimes, the bank of Grandma's will.
NARRATOR: Want to see where this road ends? Southern California. In the third quarter, the California Association of Realtors found only fifteen percent of households in Los Angeles County could afford a median-priced house. Orange County, fifteen. San Diego, sixteen. Even Riverside, one of the cheaper options, barely more than one in four.
VISUAL: A velvet rope in front of a house. A bouncer (Gus) with a clipboard. A sign: "MEMBERS ONLY. INCOME $228,000+".
NARRATOR: Statewide, the typical house costs about eight hundred ninety-five thousand dollars. To afford it, you need about two hundred twenty-eight thousand a year. NARRATOR: And even with the keys, the bills keep coming. A study of more than a million mortgages by the lender Newrez found home insurance premiums rose sixty-four percent from 2021 to 2025. In parts of Florida and the Gulf Coast, insurance alone can feel like a second mortgage.
NARRATOR: So here's the "something worse". In 2008 there was pain, and then a reset. This time there might be no reset. Prices stay high, rates stay high, and a whole generation just... waits. It's not a crash on the news. It's a crisis you notice the day you realise you're forty and still can't buy your first home.

## CHAPTER 9: WHAT THAWS THE ICE (15:20-16:55)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: But frozen markets don't stay frozen forever. So what could break this one, and which way?
VISUAL: A frozen pond. Four cracks appear one by one, each labelled.
NARRATOR: One: jobs. Most owners pay on time, but a mortgage is only as safe as the paycheck behind it. If layoffs pick up, some owners must sell, and forced sellers can't wait for a better price. That's when prices really fall, especially in Florida and Texas, where sellers already outnumber buyers. And if an underwater owner like Danielle loses her job, she can't even sell her way out.
NARRATOR: Two: costs. Insurance, property tax, repairs. Even golden handcuffs pinch when everything around the loan gets more expensive.
NARRATOR: Three, the twist: what if rates suddenly fall? You'd think that fixes everything. Maybe not.
SKIT (the musical-chairs hall again. The door bursts open: a hundred MARCUSES pile in at once, all sprinting for the same three chairs.)
LINDA (calmly, to Danielle): Well. That escalated.
NARRATOR: Millions of would-be buyers have been waiting for years. If rates drop, they could rush in at once. And builders, after years of discounting, have been slowing construction. So that crowd could run into even fewer homes. The result? Possibly a new bidding war, and prices even higher. High rates froze the market. Low rates might not thaw it. They might set off a stampede.
NARRATOR: Four, the slowest and maybe the biggest: time. Baby boomers own a huge share of America's homes. Over the next decade or two, millions will come up for sale, not because the market improved, but because life happens. Downsizing. Moving near grandkids. Health. Divorce. Death. A house one family lived in for forty years, listed for the first time.
NARRATOR: Which raises a question nobody seems to have a good answer for. When all those homes come up, who buys them? Young buyers are priced out, and at seven percent it's hard for investors to make a rental pay. The maths only works if something gives: incomes rise a lot, rates fall a lot, or prices come down. Most likely, some of all three.
NARRATOR: Which tells you the real problem was never interest rates. It's that America doesn't have enough affordable homes in the places people actually want to live.

## YOUR WALLET: FOUR QUESTIONS (16:55-17:50)
[Chapter card. VISUAL: Stromi-style index cards pinned one by one on a corkboard.]

NARRATOR: So what do you actually do with this? If you're thinking of buying or selling, ask four questions first.
NARRATOR: One. What's happening in your city, not the country? Look up whether your area is a buyer's or seller's market, and watch asking prices, because they move first. In Miami and Austin, buyers have real leverage. In parts of the Northeast, sellers still do.
NARRATOR: Two. Can you afford the whole bill, not just the mortgage? A house is only a good deal if you can still pay for it when the insurance renewal arrives.
NARRATOR: Three. Buying new? Ask the builder what the house costs without the rate buydown. That's what it's really worth, and what it might be worth if you ever need to sell.
NARRATOR: Four. If someone offers to cover your down payment, read the exit rules. Ask what happens if you need to sell, move or refinance in year three. That's when free money stops being free.
