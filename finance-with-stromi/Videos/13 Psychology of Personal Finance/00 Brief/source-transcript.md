Where the money comes from, what we've set it aside for, and how we feel about the decision can all affect what we do with it. Three, the money cost that never appears on a receipt. Let's say you have $5,000 available. In economics, opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up when making a choice. And understanding it can fundamentally change the way you evaluate financial decisions. Four, why feeling comfortable with risk doesn't mean you can afford it. Imagine you're considering an investment that could grow significantly over time but might also lose value. You believe in the investment. You've researched the company or fund. You're comfortable with the possibility of market fluctuations. So, you conclude that you're willing to take the risk. But there's another question you need to answer. If things go badly, can your finances absorb the loss? Risk tolerance is about your willingness to accept uncertainty and potential losses. Risk capacity is about your financial ability to withstand those losses without jeopardizing important obligations or goals. And confusing the two can lead to serious problems. You might be emotionally comfortable with investment risk while having very little financial room to absorb a loss. Even if that person feels confident about the investment, a sudden decline could create a problem that cannot simply be solved by waiting for a recovery. The difference is not necessarily intelligence. It's the amount of flexibility each person has. And the principle applies outside the stock market. Think about leaving a job to start a business. The decision may be reasonable if you have carefully assessed the business opportunity, established sufficient reserves, and considered your financial obligations.
