An original Stromi script for Finance video 13, "The Psychology of Personal Finance | Build a System That Works for You" (your exact title). The transcript was research only: story, cast, examples, skits and every line are new, and the source host's name and calls to action are gone. Hattie, a streetcar driver who is "good with money", spends a $1,200 bonus on a giant espresso machine while $3,000 sits in savings and $3,000 sits on a 22% card. We fall into her head: a 1930s office building with five departments, one per chapter. Skit lines in red sit between narration like Two Thursdays and Frozen Market. Circular ending: the next $1,200 bonus arrives, and this time Head Office has a rule for it. Kept apart from videos 10, 11, 16, 21 and 22: this one is about how you decide, not what to buy.

P17Switchboard floor (new)4:34

Narrator Here's the problem. Hattie makes the same small decisions over and over. Should I save this payday? How much? Should I check my account? Each one is tiny. P18Switchboard floor (new)4:58

A dusty lab-coat sign on the wall: WILLPOWER BATTERY? with a big question mark. Pip taps it and the battery falls off its hook.

Narrator You might have heard that every decision drains a fixed battery of willpower. Scientists have tested that idea hard, and the big repeat studies came back much weaker than the headlines. So nobody's saying your brain runs out. But re-deciding the same thing is still slow, still stressful, and still gives your worst mood a vote.

Pip So the battery's a myth?

Opal Tell that to my arms.

P19Switchboard floor (new)5:24

A small brass door opens. In marches Otto, a wind-up clockwork helper with a big key in his back. He takes the SAVE THIS PAYDAY? cable from Opal, plugs it into a slot marked AUTOMATIC, and the light turns a calm green. Opal slumps in relief.

Narrator Meet Otto. Otto doesn't think. Otto just does what you decided, once, on a good day.

P20Switchboard floor (new)5:39

Chalkboard on wire, two simple bars appear: OPT IN: 37% joined, AUTOMATIC: 86% joined. Then a second board: 2025, VOLUNTARY 64%, AUTO-ENROLLED 94%. Otto stands proudly next to the tall bars.

Narrator This isn't just a cute trick. In a famous study from 2001, one company switched its retirement plan so new workers were signed up automatically unless they said no. Before, about thirty seven percent of new hires joined. After, about eighty six percent did. Same people, same pay. The only thing that changed was the default. And it still holds today: in Vanguard's 2025 data, plans that enrol people automatically get about ninety four percent taking part, against sixty four percent when you have to sign up yourself.

Dot So the trick to being good with money is... being lazy in the right direction?

P21Switchboard floor (new)6:19

Narrator The trick is to split your calls in two. Big calls, like how much to save or whether to change jobs, deserve real thought. Make them slowly, once in a while. Routine calls, like moving money on payday or paying the electric bill, follow from decisions you already made. Hand those to Otto.

Opal So I only take the important calls now?

Narrator And you get a lunch break.

P22Switchboard floor (new)6:45

Narrator Try it this week. Look back at last month and find one money question you kept asking yourself. Turn it into a rule. Specific enough that you know what to do. Loose enough that you can change it when life changes.

P33Circus high wire (new)10:24

Narrator It works the other way too. Someone with a steady job, a cushion and money they won't need for decades has a big net, even if wobbles make their stomach flip. For them the danger is getting so scared they jump off the wire at the bottom, and sell right when prices are cheapest.

Hattie So the net matters more than the nerves.

Narrator Both matter. But only one of them pays the rent.

P34Circus high wire (new)10:52

Ace climbs down. On a ringmaster's easel, three questions appear one by one, each with a little icon (a cracked floor, an hourglass, a domino): 1. WHAT IF IT GOES WORSE THAN I THINK? 2. HOW LONG CAN I LAST IF IT DOESN'T GET BETTER? 3. WOULD I HAVE TO SELL, BORROW OR GIVE UP SOMETHING ELSE?

Narrator So, the third fix. Before any big risk, a new investment, quitting a job, starting a business, ask three questions. What happens if it goes worse than I expect? Narrator Now THAT is a good idea.

Top floor: a wood-panelled room with a huge blank brass plaque on the wall titled HATTIE'S HOUSE RULES. Hattie at a big typewriter, Mr. Pigeonhole, Opal, Otto, What-Else and Ace all crowded round.

Narrator On the top floor, the whole staff meets. Because every fix so far has the same shape. Make the decision once, calmly, write it down, and stop re-arguing with yourself. Put enough of those together and you get your own house rules. Think of it as an operating system for your money.

Hattie You get a rule that says no calls about the balance.

P38House Rules room (new)12:09

The typewriter clacks and a line stamps itself onto the plaque: 1. EVERY DOLLAR GETS A JOB BEFORE THE MONTH STARTS. A pie chart cut from paper unfolds: NEEDS 50, WANTS 30, SAVINGS & DEBT 20, then the slices wobble and resize to Hattie's own: 58 / 22 / 20.

Narrator Rule one: where the money goes. A famous starting point is fifty thirty twenty: half on needs, thirty percent on wants, twenty on saving and paying off debt. It was popularised by Elizabeth Warren and her daughter in a 2005 book. But it's a starting point, not a law. Hattie's rent is high, so her needs are bigger. The point is that every dollar gets a job before the month begins, not after it's gone.

Mr. Pigeonhole Jobs for dollars! I can label those!

P39House Rules room (new)12:41

Line 2 stamps on: 2. CUSHION FIRST. A small padded pillow labelled $1,000 grows, month by month, into a big mattress labelled 3 MONTHS OF BILLS. Line 3 stamps on: 3. DEBT LIST: BIGGEST INTEREST RATE FIRST. A short list unrolls: CARD 22%, CAR LOAN 7%, with the card circled in red.

Narrator Rule two: a cushion first, built a bit at a time, up to a few months of essential bills. How big depends on how steady your income is and who depends on you. Rule three: list every debt with its interest rate, and know which one is costing you the most. For Hattie, that's the card. It's not even close.

What-Else Ooooh. I'm haunting that card balance. Ooooh.

P40House Rules room (new)13:07

Line 4 stamps on: 4. NEW MONTHLY PAYMENT? TOTAL IT FIRST. A small shiny car rolls in with a friendly tag: ONLY $450 A MONTH! Otto flips the tag. On the back: x 72 MONTHS = $32,400, PLUS INSURANCE, FUEL, REPAIRS.

Narrator Rule four: before signing up for any new monthly payment, work out the total. 'Only four hundred and fifty a month' sounds small. Over six years, that's thirty two thousand four hundred dollars, before insurance and gas. Small payments are how big costs sneak in the side door.

Ace It has a cup holder that's also a cup holder.

Otto Thirty two thousand four hundred. Tick tock.

P41House Rules room (new)13:32

Line 5 stamps on: 5. DON'T UNDERSTAND IT? DON'T BUY IT. Ace sheepishly pulls a flyer out of his cape: GUARANTEED 40% A MONTH!!! and feeds it into a paper shredder. Line 6 stamps on: 6. REVIEW THE RULES EVERY MONTH.

Narrator Rule five: before putting money into anything unfamiliar, know how it works, what it costs, how you could lose, and how fast you can get your money out. If someone promises a guaranteed huge return, that's not an investment. That's a warning sign. And rule six, the one that keeps the rest alive: the rules get reviewed. They're not a contract with your past self. When life changes, you change the rules on purpose, not by accident.
