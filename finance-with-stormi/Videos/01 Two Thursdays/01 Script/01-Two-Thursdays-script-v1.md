Written 12 Nov 2026, ~17:45 UTC. Based on the story in Pushpam's transcript (the 5 Nov 2026 AI-stock crash), rewritten in our own words (tone guide: copy the formula, never the wording), plus everything that happened 6-12 Nov 2026 and what it means for markets.
Facts and sources: `01-Two-Thursdays-sources.md` in this folder. Lines marked **[CHECK AT RECORD]** depend on news still moving on 12 Nov (US market was still open when this was written).

- Target length: ~16-17 min (~2,450 words of VO + skits at ~150 wpm)
- Format: 45 s cold open, 7 chapters, narrator-to-skit loop (narrator never > 45 s straight), ending callback
- Cast: rubber-hose kit placeholders (style thread). Real people appear only as quote cards / newspaper clippings read by the narrator, never acted by our characters.
- Voice: warm documentary narrator + separate skit voices. Few black text cards: chapter titles only.

## CHAPTER 1: THE SEVEN-SLICE PIE (0:45-2:30)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: That day, Nvidia closed at the highest price in its history. At the start of autumn it was worth about five point four trillion dollars. To picture that: if the whole S&P 500 were a dollar bill, about eight cents of it was one chip company.
VISUAL: A dollar bill on a table; a pair of scissors snips off an 8-cent strip stamped NVIDIA.

NARRATOR: Here's the part most people miss. If you own a plain index fund, the safe, boring kind your uncle tells you to buy, you own a lot of Nvidia. Add Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Tesla, the so-called Magnificent Seven, and roughly a third of that fund sits on just seven companies.

SKIT (kitchen. MABEL proudly slices a pie labelled "MY INDEX FUND". She cuts 500 tiny slivers. Then she notices seven slices are enormous.)
MABEL: Five hundred companies. I'm diversified.
PIP (leaning in, counting): One, two... seven of them are a third of the pie.
MABEL (pause, quietly pushes the pie away): I'll have the salad.

NARRATOR: Even at the peak of the dot-com bubble, the market wasn't leaning this hard on so few names. And nearly all seven are betting on the same thing: artificial intelligence. That bet is enormous. This year the four biggest cloud companies plan to spend more than seven hundred billion dollars on data centers. Next year, more than a trillion. The biggest item on that shopping list? Nvidia chips. Its last quarter brought in ninety-six billion dollars of revenue, more than double a year earlier.
VISUAL: A shopping receipt unrolling off a table and down a staircase. Top line: "NVIDIA CHIPS".

NARRATOR: Everything looked perfect. But by late October, investors had started noticing cracks.

## CHAPTER 2: FOUR CRACKS (2:30-5:30)
[Chapter card. VISUAL motif: a giant green chip on a pedestal; each crack appears on it with a glass-crack SFX.]

**Crack one: the money goes in a circle**
NARRATOR: Nvidia invests in its own customers. It puts money into AI labs and chip-rental companies, and those companies then buy... Nvidia chips. It owns part of CoreWeave, which rents out Nvidia chips, and agreed to buy up to six point three billion dollars of CoreWeave's computing power if nobody else does. Even Nvidia's finance chief admitted some people would call this "circular financing."

SKIT (WENDELL in a top hat hands GUS a fat envelope of cash.)
WENDELL: A gift. Spend it on anything you like.
GUS (excited): Anything?
WENDELL (slides a catalogue across the desk; every page is the same chip): Anything.
GUS (flipping pages, slower each time): ...These are all the same.
WENDELL: Great choice.

**Crack two: the chips are the collateral**
NARRATOR: Chip-rental firms borrowed huge amounts of money to buy chips, then pledged those same chips to borrow even more. At one point, CoreWeave owed more than five dollars for every one dollar it actually owned. That works as long as a used chip keeps its value. But Nvidia launches a new, faster generation every year or two, and each new one makes the old one worth less. Think of buying a phone on credit, then using that phone as the deposit for the next loan, the same week the new model comes out.
VISUAL: A pawn shop. A chip on the counter. A price tag flips: $30,000... $20,000... $12,000.
NARRATOR: The investor Michael Burry, famous for calling the 2008 housing crash, argues the big tech companies pretend their servers last longer than they really do, which makes profits look bigger. The nightmare: one borrower misses one payment, the lender grabs the chips to sell, and the whole market learns in an afternoon what a used AI chip is really worth. It hasn't happened. But every chip investor has thought about it.

**Crack three: who's actually paying**
NARRATOR: By the end of July, customers owed Nvidia about sixty-three billion dollars for chips already delivered. And just three buyers made up more than two of every five dollars of its sales.
SKIT (diner. DOT the waitress, notepad. Three tables are piled with plates to the ceiling; every other table is empty.)
DOT (to the cook): Table one wants forty more. On the tab.
COOK: And table two?
DOT: Also on the tab.
COOK (looks at the empty room): ...Who's paying for any of this?
DOT (shrugs, pins another ticket): Future us.

**Crack four: the plug**
NARRATOR: You can buy a chip. You can't buy electricity that doesn't exist. In northern Virginia, the data-center capital of the world, a new building could wait around seven years to get connected to the grid. And the power that does exist, somebody pays for. On America's biggest power grid, which covers thirteen states, households were paying an estimated twenty-five to thirty dollars more a month than in 2024, and the grid's own watchdog pointed at data centers first.
SKIT (night, suburban kitchen. PIP opens an electric bill. The number on it is so big the paper keeps unfolding off the table. Through the window, a giant glowing data center hums next door.)
PIP: Why is the bill up again?
(The data center's lights blink twice, friendly.)
DATA CENTER (deep, cheerful hum-voice): Hi, neighbour!
NARRATOR: Nobody reads a tech company's spending report. Everybody reads their power bill. Remember that. It comes back.

NARRATOR: Wall Street had its own problems. On October twenty-ninth, Google had its worst trading day ever. Chip stocks slid into a bear market. Apple, the "boring" one, passed Nvidia in value. By early November, the biggest AI and chip stocks had lost around five trillion dollars from the top. That's about what the entire dot-com crash wiped out. That took two and a half years. This took four weeks.

## CHAPTER 3: THE BALLOT BOX (5:30-7:45)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: Then came election day. Tuesday, the third of November. Democrats won the House. Then the Senate. But the vote that mattered most to Wall Street was further down the ballot. Across ten states there were forty-one local votes on data centers. Voters went against the data centers in forty of them.
SKIT (a long table of ballot boxes. A clerk, GUS, opens each one and stamps the result: "NO". "NO". "NO". The stamp gets faster. Montage, 3 s.)
GUS (stamping, bored): No. No. No. No...
(One box at the end. He opens it. A tiny flag pops out.)
GUS: ...Yes? (squints at the label) Harwood, North Dakota.
(Distant tiny cheer from inside the box.)

NARRATOR: That's the power bill, showing up at the ballot box. In a CNBC voter poll, more than half of voters said AI and data centers were a big factor in how they voted. In Ohio, Sherrod Brown won his Senate seat back by saying, quote, "I do not work for data centers." In Texas, Gina Hinojosa, who promised to ban new data-center approvals until lawmakers passed protections, beat Governor Greg Abbott. And Bernie Sanders posted four words: "National data center moratorium now."
VISUAL: Newspaper clippings slam onto a corkboard one by one, red string connecting them to the electric bill from Chapter 2.

NARRATOR: Why does a town vote move Wall Street? Because a data center needs three things: land, power and a permit. All three are local. And the bigger fear was Washington. For years, AI builders were mostly left alone. Now a new Congress, elected the same night voters said no to data centers, might want rules on where the buildings go and who pays for their power.
SKIT (a construction site. A foreman, WENDELL, is unrolling giant blueprints labelled "$1 TRILLION". A tiny clerk walks up and staples one small sticky note to the corner.)
WENDELL (reads it): "...If they let you build it."
(He looks at the clerk. The clerk smiles and walks away.)
NARRATOR: No law had passed. Not one. It was the fear of a law that started the selling.

NARRATOR: And yet, on Wednesday, Wall Street threw a party. Oil prices dropped after hopes of a deal to end the war with Iran, and the Dow jumped more than fifteen hundred points. Its best day in over a year and a half. Traders loved cheap oil. They just hadn't thought about the new Congress yet.
[SFX: party horn, confetti]

## CHAPTER 4: THURSDAY, MINUTE BY MINUTE (7:45-10:15)
[Chapter card: "THURSDAY 5 NOVEMBER". Visual motif: a big wall clock that the camera returns to at each time stamp. No black cards; times appear on the clock.]

NARRATOR: Before the opening bell, Reuters dropped another problem. About six hundred thousand of Nvidia's H200 chips were sitting in Hong Kong, waiting for Beijing to let them into mainland China. Washington had already allowed the sales, with a twenty-five percent cut for the US government. Beijing just... hadn't said yes. Some Chinese tech giants got around ten thousand chips each. The rest waited at the door.
SKIT (a customs booth at a border. A mountain of crates labelled H200 behind GUS, a delivery guy. DOT is the customs officer, reading a newspaper.)
GUS: Washington said I can sell these!
DOT (not looking up): Then sell them.
GUS: To you! They're for you!
DOT (turns a page): Not today.
GUS: Tomorrow?
DOT (slowly lowers the paper, stares, raises it again).
NARRATOR: Nvidia had already taken a four-hundred-million-dollar hit on H200 chips nobody bought. Now the ones that had buyers couldn't get through the door.

CLOCK: 9:30.
NARRATOR: The bell rings in New York. In about six minutes, the Nasdaq gives back the entire Wednesday rally.
[SFX: opening bell, then a deflating balloon]

CLOCK: 10:35.
NARRATOR: The Nasdaq is down four percent. Across the S&P 500, two trillion dollars has disappeared, and more than four hundred billion of it is Nvidia alone.

CLOCK: 11:17.
NARRATOR: The VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, is rocketing. Think of it as the price of crash insurance. Right now, everybody wants some.
VISUAL: An insurance office; a queue of panicking investors out the door; the price board flips higher.

CLOCK: 2:58 p.m.
NARRATOR: This is where regular people come in. Maybe you're at work, checking your phone under the desk. By now, small investors have sold, on net, three hundred and fifty-eight million dollars of stock. On pace for the most since March 2020.
SKIT (office cubicle. MABEL checks her phone under the desk. Her thumb hovers between two buttons: "BUY THE DIP" and "SELL THE DIP".)
MABEL (whisper): Buy the dip...
(thumb drifts)
MABEL: ...sell the dip?
(Her boss walks past. She drops the phone into a drawer. The drawer says "SELL" in a little speech bubble.)

NARRATOR: So who was really selling? Nomura strategist Charlie McElligott pointed at hedge funds. 2026 had been a fantastic year for them, and with two months left, they sold their biggest winners to lock in the profit. Computer-driven funds and funds running on borrowed money were forced to sell too. And almost nobody stepped up to buy.
SKIT (yacht deck. Two fund managers in sunglasses, WENDELL and GUS.)
WENDELL: Great year. Let's bank it.
GUS: Sell what?
WENDELL: The winners.
GUS: Which winners?
WENDELL (sips): Yes.

CLOCK: 4:00 p.m.
NARRATOR: Closing bell. The Dow lost eighteen hundred and thirty-three points. The S&P 500 fell more than four percent, its worst second day after a midterm election in nearly a century. The Nasdaq fell five and a half percent. And the chip index lost twelve percent, its second-biggest drop on record.
VISUAL: The green chip on its pedestal from Chapter 2. All four cracks connect. A big chunk falls off.
NARRATOR: Nothing had broken. No bank failed. No law passed. Fear did it.

## CHAPTER 5: THE WEEK AFTER (10:15-13:00)  [NEW: events of 6-12 Nov]
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: That could have been the end of the story. It wasn't.

NARRATOR: Friday, tech bounced a little. Monday, it bounced a lot: the Dow up more than eleven hundred points. Then Tuesday, a new problem. Inflation came in hotter than expected, and traders started betting the Federal Reserve would raise interest rates again. Nvidia was the biggest drag on the whole market that day.
VISUAL: A rollercoaster car with our cast in it: up, down, up, then a hand labelled "INFLATION" yanks the lever.

NARRATOR: That evening, a surprise. CoreWeave, the same chip-rental company from crack number two, reported its results. Revenue up a hundred and sixty-eight percent. Orders waiting to be filled: a hundred and thirty-two and a half billion dollars. It raised its forecast for the year.
SKIT (diner from crack three. DOT pins up a new ticket. The tab board behind her is now taller than the building.)
COOK: More orders?
DOT: A hundred and thirty-two billion dollars' worth.
COOK: Paid?
DOT: ...Orders.
NARRATOR: On Wednesday, the AI trade woke back up. Nvidia rose almost three percent. AMD rose more than seven. For one day, it looked like the worst was over.

NARRATOR: But Washington hadn't forgotten. Senator Josh Hawley became the first Senate Republican to back a nationwide pause on new data centers, until Congress guarantees families won't pay for them in their power bills. Two Democrats opened an investigation into how Nvidia chips were approved for export to Chinese government-linked companies. Still no law. But the list of people with a hand near the brake was getting longer.

NARRATOR: And all week, a different story had been growing in the background. Reports said US intelligence suspected a Chinese businesswoman who had a relationship with Nvidia's CEO, Jensen Huang, of spying for Beijing. On Sunday, Huang apologised publicly for being, in his words, "unfaithful to my wife." He denied ever compromising national security or Nvidia's secrets. On Tuesday, Nvidia's board hired an outside law firm to investigate. On Wednesday, The New York Times reported a second relationship, with a senior Nvidia executive.
VISUAL: Newspaper front pages stacking up on a doormat, one per day, the pile getting taller. Keep it sober: no comic acting on this beat.

NARRATOR: Then, this morning. Thursday, the twelfth of November. Exactly one week after the crash.
VISUAL: The empty office from the cold open. The leather jacket on the chair. Silence. [SFX: single clock tick]
NARRATOR: Nvidia's board announced that Jensen Huang, who co-founded the company and ran it for thirty-three years, had resigned as CEO and left the board, effective immediately. The company's statement gave no reason. Its finance chief, Colette Kress, is now interim CEO, while still keeping her finance job. A longtime board member, Harvey Jones, became the board's first-ever chair.
NARRATOR: Nvidia shares dropped more than ten percent at the open, and the company slipped below four trillion dollars in value. Since its October peak, it has lost around two trillion dollars. **[CHECK AT RECORD: final close + market-cap figure for 12 Nov]**

SKIT (the janitor from the cold open, now holding the leather jacket on a hanger. KRESS-style interim boss is NOT drawn as a real person: show only a hand reaching in from off-screen to take two name badges, "CFO" and "CEO", and pin both on one lapel.)
JANITOR (watching the lapel): Two badges?
VOICE (off-screen, calm): Just until we find someone.
JANITOR: ...Want the jacket too?
VOICE: No.

NARRATOR: Here's the twist nobody expected. A week ago, small investors were dumping stocks. This morning, in the first thirty minutes after the news, they bought a net hundred and eleven million dollars of Nvidia. More than forty times a normal open.
VISUAL: Callback to Mabel's cubicle. This time her thumb goes straight to "BUY".

## CHAPTER 6: WHAT HAPPENS TO THE MARKET NOW (13:00-15:00)
[Chapter card. VISUAL motif: a detective's corkboard titled "WHAT TO WATCH"; each item gets pinned as the narrator names it.]

NARRATOR: So what does all this mean for the market? Five things to watch.

NARRATOR: One. Tuesday, the seventeenth of November. Nvidia reports its earnings, and the company says that date hasn't moved. It's the first big test without its founder. If sales are still growing fast, the "nothing broke" story gets stronger. If they wobble, the whole AI trade wobbles with it. Some analysts have already cut their targets; Baird halved its price target.
NARRATOR: Two. The leadership question. Investors call this "key-person risk": when one person is so tied to a company that losing them changes how much the company is worth. Some analysts called the exit a "clearing event", bad news finally out in the open. Others, like KeyBanc, warned that replacing Huang's vision will be hard. Huang has also agreed to testify before Congress before Thanksgiving, so the headlines aren't over. **[CHECK AT RECORD: testimony date]**
SKIT (a ship's wheel on a stormy deck. DOT grabs it from an empty captain's chair.)
GUS (sliding across the wet deck): Can you steer this?
DOT: I've been doing the maths for this ship for ten years.
GUS: That's not a yes!
DOT (spins the wheel hard): It's not a no.
NARRATOR: Three. China. Six hundred thousand H200 chips are still waiting in Hong Kong. And now Washington is asking how those exports were approved in the first place. Nvidia says China H200 sales were under one percent of its data-center revenue, so this is less about this quarter and more about whether the world's second-biggest market stays shut.
NARRATOR: Four. Interest rates. Hot inflation means the Fed may raise rates. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, and remember crack two: this whole build-out runs on borrowed money.
NARRATOR: Five. Washington. A lame-duck Senate, a new Democratic Congress in January, bipartisan talk of a data-center pause, and an investigation into chip exports. Every rule that slows the build-out is a smaller order for chips.
NARRATOR: And one more thing, coming fast. Anthropic, one of the biggest AI labs, is set to start trading on the stock market on the nineteenth of November, and Nvidia plans to invest ten billion dollars in it right after. Remember crack one, the money going in a circle? Here it comes again, this time in public. **[CHECK AT RECORD: IPO date + Nvidia amount]**

## CHAPTER 7: YOUR WALLET (15:00-16:10)
[Chapter card]

NARRATOR: It's easy to think this only matters to billionaires. It doesn't. Around seventy million Americans save in a 401(k), mostly through the same index funds with that big seven-slice pie. Teachers' and firefighters' pensions hold them too. When the giants fall, those accounts fall with them.
VISUAL: The pie from Chapter 1. One of the big slices shrinks; the whole pie dish tilts.
NARRATOR: Here's a simple way to picture it. If a company makes up about seven percent of your fund and its stock drops ten percent, your whole fund drops about seven tenths of one percent from that one company alone. On a hundred thousand dollars saved, that's around seven hundred dollars, from one headline, on one morning. (Illustration only; your fund's exact share changes every day.)
NARRATOR: It goes deeper than stocks. Data centers run on borrowed money, and a lot of it comes from insurers and pension funds, which means from savers. So your retirement may be quietly lending money to a half-built building.
NARRATOR: And some people get hit without owning a single share: the electricians and builders on data-center jobs, and the diners that feed them. If the spending slows, it lands on them first.
SKIT (split screen. Left: a 25-year-old, PIP, at a shop window with a "SALE" sign. Right: a 62-year-old, WENDELL, at a kitchen table with a retirement calendar.)
PIP (excited): Everything's cheaper!
WENDELL (circling a date in spring, tired): Everything's cheaper.
NARRATOR: If you're twenty-five, this was a sale. If you're about to retire, it was a very long week. (This channel explains the news. It isn't financial advice.)

## ENDING: THE PIN (16:10-16:55)

NARRATOR: So, was this the AI bubble popping? Not yet. A bubble doesn't pop when prices fall. It pops when the money stops. And the money hasn't stopped. CoreWeave just raised its forecast. The giants still plan to spend more than a trillion dollars next year.
NARRATOR: But look at what changed in two Thursdays. Voters showed they'll vote against the buildings. Washington showed it wants a say. Beijing kept the door shut. And the company at the centre of it all lost the only CEO it has ever had.
VISUAL: The green chip on its pedestal, now patched with tape. Slowly, from four directions, four hands reach into frame, each holding a pin: a VOTER's hand, a CONGRESS hand, a BEIJING hand, and an empty hand with a leather-jacket cuff, pulling away.
NARRATOR: Thursday the fifth wasn't the pop. It was the day the market noticed the pin. Thursday the twelfth was the day it learned how many hands are on it.
VISUAL: Callback to the cold open. The empty office. The janitor finishes mopping, flicks off the desk lamp. The phone lights up one more time.
NARRATOR: Next Tuesday, Nvidia tells the world how much money is still coming in. For the first time in thirty-three years, someone else will be holding the microphone.
[SFX: lamp click. Silence. End screen music starts.]
