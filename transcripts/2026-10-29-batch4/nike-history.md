# The ENTIRE History of Nike in 20 Minutes

Source: Pushpam upload, batch 4, 29 Oct 2026. Language: English. Words: 3226.

Look down at your feet. There is a decent chance that somewhere on your shoes, your shirt, or the bag by your door, there is a small curved mark that looks like a check. You did not see a company when you put them on this morning. You saw a feeling, fast, strong, a little bit unstoppable. And that feeling started with a college runner selling shoes out of the trunk of his car. Nike is not a shoe company that tells stories. Nike is a story that happens to sell shoes. And the scale is hard to believe. Nike brings in more than $46 billion a year, about $127 million every single day, and it owns almost none of the factories that make its shoes. The biggest sneaker company on Earth does not really make sneakers. Here is the part that should not be possible. LA Gear went bankrupt. Converse went bankrupt. and Nike bought what was left. Reebok, which once outsold Nike, has been sold off twice. Nike is still here. Same home in Oregon, same mark on the shoe, same obsession with winning. So, how did a runner and his old track coach build the biggest sports brand on Earth without owning a factory? Why did a company that paid $35 for its logo end up paying athletes billions? And why right now is the most famous sports brand in the world trying to remember how to be a sports company. This is the story of a business plan written for a college class and a lie told in a Japanese office. Of a waffle iron that never made waffles again. Of a logo bought from a student who did not love it and a name that came to someone in a dream. of a basketball player who wanted to sign with someone else and a slogan with a very dark origin. Of a scandal that nearly wrecked the brand and an intern who came back 30 years later to save it. A story of sweat, of swagger, and of a feeling you can wear. Every company is built on something. Nike was built on you, specifically on the part of you that wants to feel like an athlete, even if the only race you run is to the bus. To understand any of it, we have to go back further than the swoosh, further than Michael Jordan to a muddy track in Oregon in the 1950s and a coach who thought running shoes were far too heavy. His name was Bill Bowererman and he coached track at the University of Oregon. Bowererman had a theory. One ounce taken off a shoe over a mile of running added up to hundreds of pounds a runner no longer had to lift. So he took his athletes shoes apart in his workshop and rebuilt them by hand. His runners were his test subjects. They did not always enjoy it. Bowererman would go on to coach the United States Olympic track team at the 1972 games in Munich. One of those runners was a middle distance athlete named Phil Knight. He was good, not great, but he never forgot what Bowererman taught him. After Oregon, Knight went to business school at Stanford. And in 1962, he wrote a paper for a class. Its argument was simple. Cheap, highquality running shoes made in Japan could do to German brands like Adidas what Japanese cameras had done to German cameras. That same year, Knight did something unusual for a 24year-old. He flew to Japan and walked into the offices of Onitsuka, a shoe company in Coobe that made a running shoe called the Tiger. The executives asked which company he represented. He did not have one, so he made one up on the spot. Blue Ribbon Sports of Portland, Oregon. Hold on to that name. A company that did not exist had just asked to become an importer. It worked. Onitsuka agreed to let him sell Tiger Shoes in the Western United States. In 1964, Knight and Bowererman each put in $500, and Blue Ribbon Sports was born. Two years later, Bowererman wrote a short book called Jogging, which helped kick off the American running boom. The coach was not just making shoes, he was making customers. Knight sold the shoes himself from the trunk of his car at track meets across the Pacific Northwest. In its first year, the company sold about 1,300 pairs for roughly $8,000. Knight kept his day job as an accountant. His first full-time employee, a former runner named Jeff Johnson, wrote personal letters to customers, kept notes on their races and even their birthdays, and opened the company's first store in Santa Monica in 1966. Remember that trunk? Nike's entire business started as a man handing shoes to runners where they actually were. Bowererman kept tinkering. One morning in 1971, he was staring at his wife's waffle iron and wondered what would happen if a running shoe sle had that same grid of little squares. He poured rubber into the waffle iron. It glued itself shut. The waffle iron was ruined. The idea was not. The waffle sle gave runners grip without weight and it became one of the company's first great products. The Bowererman family had to buy a new waffle iron. Remember that waffle iron? Bowererman had already designed a lightweight cushion training shoe that Onitsuka sold as the Cortez and it became a bestseller. When the two companies split, both claimed the name. That fight ended up in court, too. By the early 1970s, the relationship with Onitsuka was falling apart. Each side suspected the other of planning to cut it out. Knight realized he needed his own brand and fast, and he needed a name and a logo. The company still did not have a name or a logo. Both would be chosen in a hurry, and one of them would cost $35. For the logo, Knight turned to a graphic design student he had met while teaching an accounting class at Portland State University. Her name was Carolyn Davidson. He asked her to design something that suggested motion and paid her $2 an hour. She built him for about 17 hours. Total cost $35. Knight looked at the curved mark she created and said something close to, "I don't love it, but maybe it will grow on me." The name came even closer to the deadline. Knight wanted to call the company Dimension 6. Nobody else liked it. Then Jeff Johnson, that first employee, said the word came to him in a dream. Nike, the Greek goddess of victory. Knight was not thrilled with that either, but the shoe boxes had to be printed. So Nike it was. You may have heard that Carolyn Davidson got only $35 for one of the most famous logos in history. That was the original fee. In 1983, after the company went public, Knight gave her a gold ring shaped like the logo set with a diamond and an envelope of Nike stock. The student who designed it for $2 an hour ended up owning a piece of it. In 1972, the first Nike branded shoes appeared at the United States Olympic trials in Eugene, Oregon, including a handmade racing shoe with a waffle soul that runners called the Moon Shoe. Money was so tight that American banks repeatedly refused to extend Nike more credit. A Japanese trading company, Nishouai, stepped in and kept financing the business when the banks would not. The split with Onitsuka turned into a lawsuit and in 1974, Blue Ribbon Sports won. Remember that Japanese company? Its descendant is still in this story. Nike's first great athlete was a runner from Oregon named Steve Prefontaine, a fierce, popular front runner who wore and promoted Nike shoes. In 1975, he died in a car crash at the age of 24. Nike's headquarters still honors him today. A couple of years later, the American government sent Nike a bill. Customs officials claimed the company owed about $25 million in import duties, more than it made in a year. Nike fought back, even running ads about fairness, and eventually settled for a fraction of that amount. In the late 1970s, an aerospace engineer named Frank Rudy came to Nike with a strange idea. Put pockets of pressurized air inside the sole of a shoe. Other companies had turned him down. Nike said yes and in 1978 launched its first shoe with an air cushion. Nike Air would become one of the most valuable names in footwear. In 1980, the company went public. Phil Knight, the accountant with shoes in his trunk, became a very rich man. In 1987, a designer who had trained as an architect, Tinker Hatfield, cut a window into the sole so people could actually see the air. It became the Air Max. Hatfield went on to design many of the most famous Air Jordans. In 1990, Nike moved into a sprawling campus in Beaverton, Oregon, where buildings are named after athletes. The company that started in a car trunk now had its own lakes and running trails. And that is the secret of Nike. Most shoe companies were built around making shoes. Nike was built around making meaning. It designs products, then pays other companies, mostly in Asia, to manufacture them, and spends its energy on what happens in your head when you see the mark. In business language, it is an asset light brand company with a moat built on athletes, emotion, and marketing. In plain language, Nike does not really sell shoes. It sells the person you imagine becoming when you lace them up. Anyone can copy a shoe. It is much harder to copy a feeling. You may have heard that Nike makes its own shoes. It barely does. Independent contract factories do. And today, about half of Nike's shoes come from Vietnam alone. And then Nike made the biggest bet in its history on a 21-year-old who did not even want to wear its shoes. By 1984, Nike had a problem. A rival called Reebok had caught a fitness craze, aerobics, and was racing past it. Nike was losing ground. So, it gambled most of its basketball marketing budget on one rookie, Michael Jordan. Inside Nike, the push came from a basketball consultant named Sunonny Vicaro, who argued that Nike should put almost everything on one player instead of spreading it across many. Jordan did not want to sign with Nike. He wanted Adidas. His parents, especially his mother, Dolores, pushed him to at least take the meeting. Nike offered him around $500,000 a year for 5 years, plus something almost unheard of at the time, a share of the sales of his own shoe. Jordan signed. The first Air Jordan arrived in 1985. You may have heard that the NBA banned the shoe and find Jordan $5,000 every time he wore it. The league did send Nike a warning about a red and black shoe breaking its uniform rules, and Nike turned the warning into an advertisement about a banned sneaker, but there is no solid evidence that Jordan was fined every single game. The Legends sold more shoes than the truth would have. Nike expected to sell a few million dollars of Air Jordans in the first years. It sold more than $100 million in the first year alone. Decades later, Hollywood made a whole movie about a shoe contract. Today, the Jordan brand alone sells around $7 billion of products a year, more than many entire shoe companies. In 1988, Nike's advertising agency, Widen and Kennedy, came up with three words: just do it. Dan Widen later said he adapted the line from the last words of a convicted murderer in Utah, spoken shortly before his execution. One of the most uplifting slogans in history began somewhere very dark. Nike followed it with ads that turned athletes into characters, like Bo Jackson playing every sport at once. By the early 1990s, Nike had passed Reebok again, and it never looked back. Within a few years, the same marketing machine that made Nike the coolest brand on Earth would turn the world against it. In the 1990s, reporters and activists began investigating the factories that made Nike products. They found low wages, punishing hours, and in some supplier factories, children. In 1996, a magazine published a photo of a young boy in Pakistan stitching soccer balls carrying the Nike mark. Protests followed on college campuses across America. In 1998, Phil Knight admitted in a speech that the Nike name had become linked with low wages, forced overtime, and abuse. Nike raised the minimum age for factory workers, began independent audits, and later published a list of its supplier factories. Critics said it was not enough. Supporters said Nike changed faster than most of the industry. The debate still continues. From here, the years fly. 1996, Nike signs a young golfer named Tiger Woods, reportedly for about $40 million. 2003, Nike buys Converse, the company behind the Chuck Taylor sneaker, which had gone bankrupt 2 years earlier, for about $35 million. The same year, Nike signs an 18-year-old LeBron James. 2004, Phil Knight steps down as chief executive and later as chairman. 2012, Nike launches Fly Knit, a shoe upper woven almost like a sock. 2015, Nike signs LeBron to a lifetime deal reportedly worth more than $1 billion. 2016, Phil Knight publishes his memoir, Shoe Dog, and it becomes a bestseller. 2017, Nike's Vaporfly running shoes help athletes break records so often that track officials later write new rules about shoe technology. 2018, Nike puts the former NFL quarterback Colin Kaepernick, known for kneeling during the national anthem, in an anniversary just do it ad. Some customers burned their Nike shoes in protest. Others bought more. One research firm reported that Nike's online sales jumped in the days after. The company's sales and stock held up and the ad won major awards. 2019, the Kenyon runner Elliot Kipchigible wearing Nike prototypes becomes the first person to run a marathon distance in under 2 hours in a special event that did not count as an official record. 2020, a former eBay chief executive, John Dano, takes over Nike. His big bet is to sell directly to customers through Nike's own apps and stores and to cut back on many of the retailers that had sold Nike for decades. For a while, it worked. Then it did not. Shoppers found other brands on the shelves Nike had left. Nike leaned heavily on old favorites, re-releasing classic shoes again and again. During the pandemic, a retro basketball shoe called the Dunk became a resale craze. Then Nike made so many of them that the craze wore off. Remember that waffle iron? The company that once poured rubber into kitchen appliances to invent something new was mostly selling its greatest hits. Newer running brands like an and Hoka grabbed runners Nike used to own. Nike's stock fell by more than half from its peak, which is why what happened next at Nike surprised almost everyone, including Nike. In October 2024, Nike brought back Elliot Hill as chief executive. Hill had joined Nike in 1988 as an intern and spent more than 30 years there before retiring in 2020. Now he was back with a plan he called the sport offense. Focus on athletes again. Win back runners, basketball players, and soccer players, and make peace with the stores Nike had walked away from, from big sneaker chains to small running shops. Remember that trunk? After years of trying to sell everything through its own app, Nike was going back to meeting customers wherever they shop. Which brings us to right now. In its fiscal year that ended in May 2026, Nike brought in about $46.4 billion, flat from the year before. Its performance business, shoes, and gear for running, soccer, and basketball, grew to about $16 billion. But the rest of the company is still struggling. In the first quarter of its new fiscal year, sales fell 4%. Sales in Greater China, about an eighth of the business, dropped 26%. Converse, the bankrupt brand Nike rescued, shrank by almost a third in a single year. Nike's own digital sales, the heart of the direct to customer plan, fell by about $1 billion. Jordan Brand, still worth around $7 billion a year, fell too. Nike deliberately cut sales of one of its most popular retro sneakers, the Dunk, by nearly half to stop the market from being flooded. and it announced a new restructuring plan called Pace with layoffs beginning in 2027. For the full year, Nike expects revenue to fall again. Hill told analysts that Nike was moving with urgency, and the company that once paid $35 for its logo still spends well over $1 billion every quarter on marketing and athletes. Still, there are signs of life. Sales in North America grew. Running, soccer, and basketball are growing again. And in the summer of 2026, the World Cup came to the United States, Canada, and Mexico with Nike spending heavily to put its shoes and kits in front of the world. And remember that Japanese company from 1962, Onitsuka eventually became part of AS6, and AS6 has spent the last few years winning over serious runners, some of them the same runners Nike lost. AS6 even still sells shoes under the old Onitsuka Tiger name. The company that gave Phil Knight his first shoes is now one of the rivals he has to beat. So why does any of this matter? Because Nike's oldest idea is being tested again. For 60 years, Nike has sold a feeling that you are an athlete, that you can win, that you should just do it. But a feeling only works if the product underneath it is good enough to believe. When Nike chased fashion and apps, runners noticed. Now it is trying to prove it can still make the best shoe on the starting line, not just the most famous one. And it matters for you. The way almost every brand talks to you today with athletes, emotion, and a story instead of a list of features was largely invented by a shoe company in Oregon. Every ad that sells you a lifestyle instead of a product owes something to Nike. So look down at your feet again. Maybe that small curved mark is there. Maybe it is not. Either way, the feeling it sells started with a coach who hated heavy shoes and a runner with a trunk full of them. A lost supplier could not stop it. Reebok could not catch it. A scandal could not finish it. Nike was never really built on shoes. It was built on a feeling and on the part of you that still wants to win. The only question left is whether Nike can make you feel that way again. Thanks for building this story with us. From a waffle iron in Oregon to a 46 billion feeling.
