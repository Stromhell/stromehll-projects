Why flamingos stand on ONE leg (it's the lazy option)

Flamingos sleep on one leg because their body locks into place with almost no muscle effort.

Caption
Standing on one leg looks hard. For a flamingo it's the easy way.
In 2017, scientists found a flamingo's body locks into a stable one-leg pose with almost no muscle work. Even a dead flamingo balanced on one leg. Two legs actually take more effort.
Sources: The Conversation (Georgia Tech / Emory study, Biology Letters 2017); Science News Explores.

12:30 PMengineering#03

Why plane windows are NEVER square

Plane windows are rounded because square corners concentrate stress, which helped break apart the de Havilland Comet in 1954.

Caption
The first jet airliner had square windows. It did not end well.
In 1954 two de Havilland Comets broke apart mid-air. Tests in a giant water tank showed cracks growing from the sharp window corners, where stress piles up. Rounded corners spread that stress out evenly.
Sources: HowStuffWorks: Why are airplane windows round?; Migflug: de Havilland Comet, metal fatigue and square windows.

2:00 PMspace#04

Mercury is closer to the Sun. So why is Venus HOTTER?

Venus is hotter than Mercury because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat.

Caption
The closest planet to the Sun is not the hottest one.
Mercury has almost no air, so its heat escapes to space. Venus is wrapped in thick carbon dioxide that traps the heat. Its surface stays above 460°C, day and night. Hot enough to melt lead.
Sources: NASA Space Place: All about Venus; BBC Sky at Night: Why is Venus the hottest planet.

5:00 PManimals#05

Woodpeckers hit trees all day. Why no concussion?

Woodpecker skulls are not shock absorbers; their tiny brains simply tolerate the jolts.

Caption
Bang your head on a tree like this and you'd be knocked out.
For years people thought a woodpecker's skull was a shock absorber. A 2022 high-speed camera study found the opposite: the head works like a stiff hammer. The real secret is size. A tiny brain can take much bigger jolts.
Sources: Smithsonian Magazine; Van Wassenbergh et al., Current Biology 2022.

Your wrinkly bath fingers are NOT just soggy skin

Bath-wrinkled fingers are switched on by your nervous system, possibly to improve grip on wet things.

Caption
Those prune fingers after a bath? Your nerves are doing that.
If the nerve to a finger is damaged, that finger stays smooth. Your nervous system shrinks blood vessels under the skin and pulls it into ridges. One idea: grip. In tests people handled wet objects better with wrinkly fingers.
Sources: Popular Science: Why do fingers wrinkle in water?; USC: Wrinkly fingertips proven useful.

7:00 PMspace / human body#07

Space slowly eats astronauts' bones. Here's why

Astronauts lose bone in space because bones rebuild based on the load they carry.

Caption
Astronauts float with no weight. So why do they come home weaker?
Your bones rebuild themselves based on the load they carry. In space your legs and hips carry almost nothing, so the body takes bone away, around 1 to 2 percent a month. That's why space station crews exercise every single day.
Sources: NASA Astrobiology: Space bones; Universe Today: NASA learns more about bone loss in space.

8:00 PMhuman body#01

Stranded at sea: why can't you drink the water?

Kidneys can't make urine as salty as seawater, so every sip costs more water than it gives.

Caption
Surrounded by water and still dying of thirst? Here's why a sip of the sea makes it worse.
Seawater is about 3.5% salt, and your kidneys can't make urine that salty. To flush the salt from one cup, you'd lose more than a cup of water.
Sources: NOAA Ocean Service, USGS Water Science School.

9:00 PMhistory / maths#08

Why does an hour have 60 minutes, not 100?

An hour has 60 minutes because the Babylonians counted in base 60, which divides evenly many ways.

Caption
100 minutes in an hour would be easier. So why 60?
Blame the Babylonians. They counted in sixties, and 60 splits evenly into halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths and tenths. Their astronomers used it to track the sky and time. We still use it for hours, minutes, seconds and the 360 degrees of a circle.
Sources: Think Academy: Why does an hour have 60 minutes?; The Star: The ancient reason there are 60 minutes in an hour.

10:00 PManimals#09

Why spiders don't get stuck in their own webs

Spiders avoid sticking to their own webs thanks to branching leg hairs, a non-stick coating and careful movement.

Caption
A web traps flies. So why doesn't the spider get stuck?
In 2012 Smithsonian researchers looked closely at spider legs. Branching hairs mean only a few tips touch the glue, and the legs carry a non-stick coating. Wash it off and the legs stick much more. Spiders also move carefully.
Sources: Smithsonian Science: Fancy footwork and non-stick leg coating; Phys.org: Why spiders do not stick to their own webs.

11:00 PMchemistry / nature#10

Ice SHOULD sink. Here's why it floats

Ice floats because water molecules lock into a roomy crystal when frozen, making ice less dense than water.

Caption
Almost everything sinks when it freezes solid. Not water.
When water freezes, its molecules lock into a roomy crystal, so ice is lighter than the same amount of water. Lakes freeze from the top down, and the ice works like a lid that keeps the water below liquid all winter. Lucky fish.
Sources: UC ANR: Why does ice float?; Why lakes and ponds freeze from the top down.

8:00 PMspace / calendar#11

Why February 29 exists (and why 1900 skipped it)

Leap years exist because Earth's orbit takes about 365.25 days; most century years skip it to fix the overshoot.

Caption
Why does February get an extra day every four years?
Earth takes about 365 and a quarter days to orbit the Sun. Skip that quarter and the calendar slips almost a day every four years, more than three weeks a century. Adding February 29 overshoots a little, so most century years skip it.
Sources: Royal Museums Greenwich: Which years are leap years?; Astronomy.com: Why do we have leap days.
